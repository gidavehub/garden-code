Chapter 1: Complex Numbers

Introduction:
Throughout our mathematical journey so far, we have primarily worked within the system of real numbers. Real numbers are sufficient for solving many types of equations and modeling various real-world phenomena. However, certain simple algebraic equations, such as x² + 1 = 0, have no solutions within the real number system because there is no real number whose square is negative. To address this limitation and expand the realm of numbers, mathematicians introduced the concept of imaginary numbers, and combining these with real numbers led to the development of complex numbers. Complex numbers provide a complete algebraic system where every polynomial equation has roots, and they have found profound applications in fields such as electrical engineering, quantum mechanics, signal processing, and fluid dynamics. This chapter will introduce the definition of complex numbers, their representation on the Argand diagram, arithmetic operations, the concepts of conjugates, modulus, and argument, and explore their polar (trigonometric) form, Euler's formula, De Moivre's theorem, and roots of unity.

1.1 Definition of Complex Numbers, Argand Diagram

The Imaginary Unit (i):
The foundation of complex numbers is the imaginary unit, denoted by the symbol i.

i is defined as the square root of -1: i = √(-1)

Therefore, i² = -1.
This definition allows us to find square roots of negative numbers. For example, √(-9) = √(9 × -1) = √9 × √(-1) = 3i.

Definition of a Complex Number (z):
A complex number z is a number that can be expressed in the form:
z = a + bi
where:

a and b are real numbers.

a is called the real part of z, denoted as Re(z) = a.

b is called the imaginary part of z, denoted as Im(z) = b.

i is the imaginary unit (i² = -1).

Examples of Complex Numbers:

3 + 2i (Real part = 3, Imaginary part = 2)

-5 + 7i (Real part = -5, Imaginary part = 7)

4 (Can be written as 4 + 0i; a purely real number, Imaginary part = 0)

6i (Can be written as 0 + 6i; a purely imaginary number, Real part = 0)

The set of all complex numbers is denoted by ℂ. Real numbers (ℝ) are a subset of complex numbers (where b=0).

The Argand Diagram (Complex Plane):
Complex numbers can be represented geometrically as points in a two-dimensional plane called the Argand diagram or complex plane.

The horizontal axis is called the real axis (Re).

The vertical axis is called the imaginary axis (Im).

A complex number z = a + bi is represented by the point with Cartesian coordinates (a, b) in this plane.

Alternatively, the complex number z = a + bi can be represented by a vector originating from the origin (0,0) and terminating at the point (a, b).

This geometric representation is crucial for understanding the modulus, argument, and operations (especially multiplication and division in polar form) of complex numbers.

(Suggested insertion: A clear diagram of the Argand diagram, labeling the real axis (Re), imaginary axis (Im), and showing the plotting of several example complex numbers like 3+2i, -1+4i, 2-3i, -4i, 5. Illustrate one complex number also as a vector from the origin to the point.)

Equality of Complex Numbers:
Two complex numbers, z₁ = a₁ + b₁i and z₂ = a₂ + b₂i, are equal if and only if their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal.
z₁ = z₂ if and only if a₁ = a₂ AND b₁ = b₂.

1.2 Arithmetic of Complex Numbers, Conjugates, Modulus, Argument

Arithmetic Operations:
Complex numbers can be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided.

Addition: Add the real parts and the imaginary parts separately.
(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i

Example: (3 + 2i) + (1 - 4i) = (3+1) + (2-4)i = 4 - 2i.

Subtraction: Subtract the real parts and the imaginary parts separately.
(a + bi) - (c + di) = (a - c) + (b - d)i

Example: (5 - i) - (2 + 3i) = (5-2) + (-1-3)i = 3 - 4i.

Multiplication: Multiply as binomials (using FOIL or distributive property), remembering that i² = -1.
(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi²
= ac + (ad + bc)i + bd(-1)
= (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i

Example: (2 + 3i)(1 - i) = 2(1) + 2(-i) + 3i(1) + 3i(-i)
= 2 - 2i + 3i - 3i²
= 2 + i - 3(-1)
= 2 + i + 3 = 5 + i.

Complex Conjugate (z̅ or z):*
The complex conjugate of a complex number z = a + bi is z̅ = a - bi. (The sign of the imaginary part is changed).

Example: If z = 3 + 4i, then z̅ = 3 - 4i.

Example: If z = 2 - 5i, then z̅ = 2 + 5i.

If z is a real number (b=0), then z̅ = z.

Geometrically, z̅ is the reflection of z across the real axis in the Argand diagram.

Properties of Conjugates:

(z̅)̅ = z

z + z̅ = (a + bi) + (a - bi) = 2a (Always a real number)

z - z̅ = (a + bi) - (a - bi) = 2bi (Always purely imaginary if b≠0)

z z̅ = (a + bi)(a - bi) = a² - (bi)² = a² - b²i² = a² - b²(-1) = a² + b² (Always a non-negative real number. This is |z|², see Modulus).

(z₁ + z₂)̅ = z₁̅ + z₂̅

(z₁ - z₂)̅ = z₁̅ - z₂̅

(z₁ z₂)̅ = z₁̅ z₂̅

(z₁ / z₂)̅ = z₁̅ / z₂̅ (if z₂ ≠ 0)

Division: To divide complex numbers, multiply the numerator and denominator by the complex conjugate of the denominator. This makes the denominator a real number.
(a + bi) / (c + di) = [(a + bi)(c - di)] / [(c + di)(c - di)]
= [(ac - adi + bci - bdi²)] / (c² + d²)
= [(ac + bd) + (bc - ad)i] / (c² + d²)
= [(ac + bd) / (c² + d²)] + [(bc - ad) / (c² + d²)]i (Separating real and imaginary parts).

Example: (2 + i) / (1 - 3i)
Multiply numerator and denominator by (1 + 3i):
[(2 + i)(1 + 3i)] / [(1 - 3i)(1 + 3i)] = [2 + 6i + i + 3i²] / [1² + 3²]
= [2 + 7i - 3] / [1 + 9] = [-1 + 7i] / 10 = -1/10 + (7/10)i.

Modulus of a Complex Number (|z|):
The modulus of a complex number z = a + bi is its distance from the origin (0,0) in the Argand diagram. It is a non-negative real number.
|z| = |a + bi| = √(a² + b²)
(This is derived from the Pythagorean theorem, as |z| is the length of the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides a and b).

|z|² = z z̅ = a² + b².

Properties of Modulus:

|z| ≥ 0; |z| = 0 if and only if z = 0.

|z| = |-z| = |z̅|.

|z₁z₂| = |z₁| |z₂|.

|z₁/z₂| = |z₁| / |z₂| (if z₂ ≠ 0).

Triangle Inequality: |z₁ + z₂| ≤ |z₁| + |z₂|.

Argument of a Complex Number (arg(z) or φ or θ):
The argument of a non-zero complex number z = a + bi is the angle φ (usually in radians) that the vector representing z (from origin to point (a,b)) makes with the positive real axis in the Argand diagram.

tan(φ) = b/a (if a ≠ 0).

The argument is multi-valued, as adding 2πn (where n is an integer) gives the same direction.

Principal Argument (Arg(z)): Usually defined to be in the interval (-π, π] or [0, 2π). Care must be taken to find the correct quadrant based on the signs of a and b.

If a > 0 (Quadrants I or IV): φ = arctan(b/a).

If a < 0, b ≥ 0 (Quadrant II): φ = arctan(b/a) + π (or 180°).

If a < 0, b < 0 (Quadrant III): φ = arctan(b/a) - π (or +π then adjust, or arctan(b/a) + 180° then find principal).

If a = 0, b > 0: φ = π/2 (90°).

If a = 0, b < 0: φ = -π/2 (or 3π/2, 270°).

If z=0, argument is undefined.

1.3 Polar (Trigonometric) Form and Euler’s Formula

Representing a complex number z = a + bi using its modulus r = |z| and argument φ = arg(z).
From the Argand diagram, we can see:

a = r cos(φ)

b = r sin(φ)
So, z = a + bi = r cos(φ) + (r sin(φ))i = r(cos(φ) + i sin(φ)).
This is the polar form (or trigonometric form or modulus-argument form) of a complex number.
z = r(cos φ + i sin φ) (often abbreviated as r cis φ)
Where:

r = |z| = √(a² + b²) (modulus)

φ = arg(z) (argument), with tan(φ) = b/a and correct quadrant considered.

Multiplication and Division in Polar Form:
If z₁ = r₁(cos φ₁ + i sin φ₁) and z₂ = r₂(cos φ₂ + i sin φ₂), then:

Multiplication: Multiply the moduli and add the arguments.
z₁z₂ = r₁r₂ [cos(φ₁ + φ₂) + i sin(φ₁ + φ₂)]

Division: Divide the moduli and subtract the arguments.
z₁/z₂ = (r₁/r₂) [cos(φ₁ - φ₂) + i sin(φ₁ - φ₂)] (for z₂ ≠ 0)
These rules make multiplication and division of complex numbers (especially powers and roots) much simpler than in Cartesian form a+bi.

Euler's Formula (Leonhard Euler):
A fundamental relationship connecting complex exponentials with trigonometric functions:
e^(iφ) = cos(φ) + i sin(φ)
where e is Euler's number (base of natural logarithm, e ≈ 2.718...), and φ is in radians.

This allows the polar form to be written concisely as:
z = r e^(iφ) (Exponential form of a complex number).

From Euler's formula:
cos(φ) = (e^(iφ) + e^(-iφ)) / 2
sin(φ) = (e^(iφ) - e^(-iφ)) / (2i)

A special case: e^(iπ) = cos(π) + i sin(π) = -1 + i(0) = -1. So, e^(iπ) + 1 = 0 (Euler's Identity - links five fundamental mathematical constants).

Using exponential form for multiplication/division:
z₁z₂ = (r₁e^(iφ₁))(r₂e^(iφ₂)) = r₁r₂ e^(i(φ₁ + φ₂))
z₁/z₂ = (r₁e^(iφ₁)) / (r₂e^(iφ₂)) = (r₁/r₂) e^(i(φ₁ - φ₂))

1.4 Roots of Unity and De Moivre’s Theorem

De Moivre's Theorem (Abraham de Moivre):
A powerful theorem for finding powers and roots of complex numbers in polar form.
If z = r(cos φ + i sin φ) and n is an integer, then:
zⁿ = [r(cos φ + i sin φ)]ⁿ = rⁿ(cos(nφ) + i sin(nφ))

In exponential form: (re^(iφ))ⁿ = rⁿe^(inφ).

This theorem is very useful for calculating (cos φ + i sin φ)ⁿ and for deriving trigonometric identities (e.g., expressing cos(nφ) and sin(nφ) in terms of powers of cosφ and sinφ).

Finding n-th Roots of a Complex Number:
A non-zero complex number z = r(cos φ + i sin φ) has exactly n distinct n-th roots.
If w = ⁿ√z, and w = R(cos θ + i sin θ), then wⁿ = z.
Using De Moivre's theorem: Rⁿ(cos(nθ) + i sin(nθ)) = r(cos φ + i sin φ).
So, Rⁿ = r => R = ⁿ√r (the real, positive n-th root of r).
And nθ = φ + 2kπ (where k is an integer, because angles can repeat every 2π).
θ = (φ + 2kπ) / n.
To get n distinct roots, let k = 0, 1, 2, ..., (n-1).
The n-th roots are given by:
w_k = ⁿ√r [cos((φ + 2kπ)/n) + i sin((φ + 2kπ)/n)]
for k = 0, 1, 2, ..., (n-1).

The n distinct n-th roots all have the same modulus ⁿ√r.

They are equally spaced around a circle of radius ⁿ√r in the Argand diagram, with an angular separation of 2π/n radians (or 360°/n) between successive roots.

Roots of Unity:
The n-th roots of 1 (i.e., solutions to zⁿ = 1).
Since 1 = 1(cos 0 + i sin 0) (so r=1, φ=0), the n-th roots of unity are:
w_k = cos(2kπ/n) + i sin(2kπ/n)
for k = 0, 1, 2, ..., (n-1).

The principal root (for k=0) is w₀ = 1.

Geometrically, the n-th roots of unity lie on the unit circle in the Argand diagram and form the vertices of a regular n-sided polygon inscribed in the circle, with one vertex at (1,0).

If ω = cos(2π/n) + i sin(2π/n) is the principal complex n-th root, then the roots are 1, ω, ω², ..., ωⁿ⁻¹.

The sum of the n-th roots of unity is 0 (for n > 1).

Chapter 2: Binomial Theorem and Series Expansions
Introduction:
The Binomial Theorem provides a powerful formula for expanding expressions of the form (a + x)ⁿ for various types of exponents n. Initially developed for positive integer powers, it can be generalized to include negative and fractional exponents, leading to infinite series expansions. These expansions are not only crucial for algebraic manipulation but also have significant applications in approximations, probability, and other areas of mathematics and science. This chapter will cover the binomial expansion for different types of n, explore its applications, and introduce the concepts of Maclaurin and Taylor series as a more general way to represent functions as infinite sums.
2.1 Binomial Expansion for Positive Integer Powers (n ∈ ℕ)
When n is a positive integer, the binomial theorem provides a finite sum for the expansion of (a + x)ⁿ.
Simple Expansions:
(a + x)⁰ = 1
(a + x)¹ = a + x
(a + x)² = a² + 2ax + x²
(a + x)³ = a³ + 3a²x + 3ax² + x³
(a + x)⁴ = a⁴ + 4a³x + 6a²x² + 4ax³ + x⁴
Observations from these expansions:
There are n + 1 terms in the expansion of (a + x)ⁿ.
The powers of a decrease from n down to 0.
The powers of x increase from 0 up to n.
The sum of the powers of a and x in each term is always n.
The coefficients follow a pattern, known as binomial coefficients.
Pascal's Triangle:
A triangular array of numbers where each number is the sum of the two numbers directly above it (with 1s along the edges). The rows of Pascal's triangle give the binomial coefficients for (a + x)ⁿ.
Row 0: 1 (for n=0)
Row 1: 1 1 (for n=1)
Row 2: 1 2 1 (for n=2)
Row 3: 1 3 3 1 (for n=3)
Row 4: 1 4 6 4 1 (for n=4)
Row 5: 1 5 10 10 5 1 (for n=5)
And so on.
Binomial Coefficients (nCr or (ⁿᵣ) or C(n,r)):
The coefficient of the term a^(n-r) x^r in the expansion of (a + x)ⁿ is given by the binomial coefficient nCr ("n choose r"), which represents the number of ways to choose r items from a set of n distinct items without regard to order.
nCr = n! / [r! (n - r)!]
where n! (n factorial) = n × (n-1) × ... × 2 × 1, and 0! = 1.
nC0 = 1, nCn = 1, nCr = nC(n-r).
The Binomial Theorem (for positive integer n):
(a + x)ⁿ = nC0 aⁿx⁰ + nC1 aⁿ⁻¹x¹ + nC2 aⁿ⁻²x² + ... + nCr aⁿ⁻ʳxʳ + ... + nCn a⁰xⁿ
Or, using summation notation:
(a + x)ⁿ = Σ [from r=0 to n] (nCr aⁿ⁻ʳ xʳ)
Key terms and properties:
General Term ( (r+1)-th term, T_(r+1) ): T_(r+1) = nCr aⁿ⁻ʳ xʳ. (Note: r starts from 0 for the first term).
For the expansion of (1 + x)ⁿ:
(1 + x)ⁿ = nC0 + nC1 x + nC2 x² + ... + nCr xʳ + ... + xⁿ
= 1 + nx + [n(n-1)/2!] x² + [n(n-1)(n-2)/3!] x³ + ... + xⁿ
If the binomial is (a - x)ⁿ, then the terms alternate in sign:
(a - x)ⁿ = nC0 aⁿ - nC1 aⁿ⁻¹x + nC2 aⁿ⁻²x² - ... + (-1)ʳ nCr aⁿ⁻ʳxʳ + ... + (-1)ⁿ xⁿ
(Suggested insertion: Illustration of Pascal's triangle up to n=6. Clear definition of nCr and calculation examples. Worked examples expanding binomials like (x+2y)⁴ and (2x-1)⁵ using both Pascal's triangle and nCr. Examples of finding a specific term or coefficient in an expansion.)
2.2 Generalized Binomial Theorem for Fractional and Negative Indices
The binomial theorem can be extended to cases where the exponent n is not a positive integer (i.e., n can be negative or a fraction). In such cases, the expansion becomes an infinite series and is only valid under certain conditions.
The Generalized Binomial Theorem (for (1 + x)ⁿ where n is any real number):
If n is any real number (negative, fractional, or positive non-integer), and |x| < 1 (the condition for convergence of the series), then:
(1 + x)ⁿ = 1 + nx + [n(n-1)/2!] x² + [n(n-1)(n-2)/3!] x³ + ... + [n(n-1)...(n-r+1)/r!] xʳ + ...
Key Differences from Positive Integer Case:
The series is infinite (does not terminate).
The expansion is valid only if |x| < 1 (or -1 < x < 1). This is the condition of validity or interval of convergence.
The coefficients cannot be written using nCr notation in the same way (as n is not necessarily a positive integer greater than or equal to r). The general form of the coefficient is used.
Expansion of (a + x)ⁿ for general n:
To use the generalized theorem, first factor out a:
(a + x)ⁿ = aⁿ (1 + x/a)ⁿ
Then expand (1 + x/a)ⁿ using the series, provided |x/a| < 1.
(a + x)ⁿ = aⁿ {1 + n(x/a) + [n(n-1)/2!] (x/a)² + [n(n-1)(n-2)/3!] (x/a)³ + ...}
Common Expansions:
(1 + x)⁻¹ = 1 - x + x² - x³ + ... (Geometric series, valid for |x|<1)
(1 - x)⁻¹ = 1 + x + x² + x³ + ... (Valid for |x|<1)
(1 + x)^(1/2) = √ (1+x) = 1 + (1/2)x - (1/8)x² + (1/16)x³ - ... (Valid for |x|<1)
(1 + x)⁻² = 1 - 2x + 3x² - 4x³ + ... (Valid for |x|<1)

2.3 Applications: Approximations, Series Summation Techniques (Focused)

Approximations:

When x is small (|x| << 1), the binomial expansion provides a good way to approximate (1 + x)ⁿ by using only the first few terms of its infinite series. The accuracy generally improves with more terms.

First-order approximation: (1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx (This is often sufficient if x is very small).

Second-order approximation: (1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx + [n(n-1)/2!] x².

Procedure for Approximating expressions like (a + x)ⁿ or ⁿ√(a+x):

Rewrite the expression in the form A(1 + B)ⁿ where |B| is small. Often involves factoring out the larger part.

Apply the binomial expansion to (1 + B)ⁿ up to a desired number of terms.

Multiply by A.

Example 1: Find an approximation for √(26).
√(26) = √(25 + 1) = √(25(1 + 1/25)) = √25 * (1 + 1/25)^(1/2) = 5 * (1 + 0.04)^(1/2).
Here n=1/2, x=0.04.
5 * (1 + 0.04)^(1/2) ≈ 5 * [1 + (1/2)(0.04) + ( (1/2)(-1/2) / 2! ) (0.04)² ]
≈ 5 * [1 + 0.02 - (1/8)(0.0016)]
≈ 5 * [1 + 0.02 - 0.0002] = 5 * [1.0198] = 5.0990.
(Calculator: √26 ≈ 5.0990195).

Example 2: Approximate 1 / (1.03)³ = (1 + 0.03)⁻³.
Here n=-3, x=0.03.
≈ 1 + (-3)(0.03) + ( (-3)(-4) / 2! ) (0.03)²
≈ 1 - 0.09 + (12/2)(0.0009) = 1 - 0.09 + 6(0.0009)
≈ 1 - 0.09 + 0.0054 = 0.9154.

Validity of Expansion: Always state the range of x values for which the infinite binomial expansion is valid (converges). For (1 + y)ⁿ, the condition is |y| < 1.

2.4 Maclaurin and Taylor Series (Brief Introduction)

While the binomial theorem specifically expands expressions of the form (a+x)ⁿ, Taylor series and Maclaurin series provide a more general method for representing many well-behaved functions as infinite power series (polynomials of infinite degree).

These series allow us to approximate functions locally using polynomials, understand their behavior, and evaluate them.

Taylor Series Expansion of f(x) about x = a:
If a function f(x) has derivatives of all orders at x=a, its Taylor series is:
f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)/1! + f''(a)(x-a)²/2! + f'''(a)(x-a)³/3! + ...
= Σ [from k=0 to ∞] (f^(k)(a)/k!) (x-a)^k
where f^(k)(a) is the k-th derivative of f evaluated at a, and k! = k × (k-1) × ... × 1 (0!=1).

Maclaurin Series: A special case of the Taylor series where the expansion is about x = 0 (i.e., a = 0).
f(x) = f(0) + f'(0)x/1! + f''(0)x²/2! + f'''(0)x³/3! + ...
= Σ [from k=0 to ∞] (f^(k)(0)/k!) x^k

Key Idea: Many common functions can be represented by these series within their interval of convergence.

The generalized binomial series for (1 + x)ⁿ IS the Maclaurin series for f(x) = (1 + x)ⁿ.
Calculating the derivatives of f(x) = (1 + x)ⁿ at x=0:
f(0) = 1
f'(x) = n(1+x)ⁿ⁻¹, f'(0) = n
f''(x) = n(n-1)(1+x)ⁿ⁻², f''(0) = n(n-1)
f'''(x) = n(n-1)(n-2)(1+x)ⁿ⁻³, f'''(0) = n(n-1)(n-2)
Substituting into the Maclaurin formula gives the binomial expansion.

Common Maclaurin Series (often given or derived in early calculus):
(For x in radians for trig functions)

e^x = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + ... (for all x)

sin(x) = x - x³/3! + x⁵/5! - x⁷/7! + ... (for all x)

cos(x) = 1 - x²/2! + x⁴/4! - x⁶/6! + ... (for all x)

ln(1 + x) = x - x²/2 + x³/3 - x⁴/4 + ... (for -1 < x ≤ 1)

These series are fundamental in advanced mathematics for approximating functions, solving differential equations, and in theoretical physics. For this course, familiarity with the concept and perhaps derivation of the binomial expansion as a Maclaurin series is typical.

3.1 Polynomial Division, Factor Theorem, and Remainder Theorem

Polynomial (Review): An expression of the form P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + ... + a_1 x + a_0, where a_i are real coefficients, a_n ≠ 0, and n is a non-negative integer (the degree of the polynomial).

Polynomial Division: Similar to long division of numbers, we can divide one polynomial by another (non-zero) polynomial.
If P(x) is a polynomial (dividend) and D(x) is a non-zero polynomial (divisor) with degree(D(x)) ≤ degree(P(x)), then there exist unique polynomials Q(x) (quotient) and R(x) (remainder) such that:
P(x) = D(x) Q(x) + R(x)
where either R(x) = 0 or degree(R(x)) < degree(D(x)).

If R(x) = 0, then D(x) is a factor of P(x).

The process is analogous to numerical long division.

Synthetic Division (Special Case): A shorthand method for dividing a polynomial P(x) by a linear binomial of the form (x - c). It is quicker than long division but only applicable for linear divisors of this specific form.

Remainder Theorem:
If a polynomial P(x) is divided by a linear binomial (x - c), the remainder R is equal to P(c) (the value of the polynomial when x = c).

P(x) = (x - c) Q(x) + R
Substituting x = c: P(c) = (c - c) Q(c) + R = 0 ⋅ Q(c) + R = R.

Useful for finding the remainder without performing the full division, or for evaluating P(c).

Factor Theorem:
A direct consequence of the Remainder Theorem.
A linear binomial (x - c) is a factor of a polynomial P(x) if and only if P(c) = 0 (i.e., if c is a root or zero of the polynomial).

If P(c) = 0, then (x - c) is a factor of P(x).

If (x - c) is a factor of P(x), then P(c) = 0.

Useful for finding factors of polynomials and thus solving polynomial equations. If one root c is known (or found by trial and error, often testing factors of the constant term), then (x-c) is a factor, and polynomial division can be used to find other factors.

3.2 Roots of Polynomials: Nature, Multiplicity, and Complex Roots

Roots (or Zeros) of a Polynomial: The values of x for which P(x) = 0. These correspond to the x-intercepts of the graph of y = P(x).

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (stated): Every non-constant single-variable polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root. A corollary is that a polynomial of degree n has exactly n complex roots, counting multiplicities.

Nature of Roots:
For a polynomial with real coefficients:

Real Roots: Values of x where the graph y=P(x) crosses or touches the x-axis.

Complex Roots (Imaginary Roots): Roots that are not real numbers; they involve the imaginary unit i.

If a polynomial with real coefficients has a complex root a + bi (where b ≠ 0), then its complex conjugate a - bi must also be a root. Complex roots with non-zero imaginary parts always occur in conjugate pairs for polynomials with real coefficients.

Multiplicity of a Root:
If (x - c)^k is a factor of P(x) but (x - c)^(k+1) is not, then c is a root of multiplicity k.

If k = 1, it is a simple root (graph crosses x-axis).

If k is even (e.g., k=2, double root), the graph touches the x-axis at x=c but does not cross it (tangent to x-axis).

If k is odd and k > 1 (e.g., k=3, triple root), the graph crosses the x-axis at x=c but flattens out there (point of inflection on x-axis).

Finding Roots:

For linear polynomials (ax+b=0): x = -b/a.

For quadratic polynomials (ax²+bx+c=0): Quadratic formula x = [-b ± √(b²-4ac)] / 2a. Discriminant b²-4ac determines nature of roots.

For higher-degree polynomials:

Rational Root Theorem: If a polynomial with integer coefficients has rational roots p/q (in simplest form), then p must be a factor of the constant term (a_0) and q must be a factor of the leading coefficient (a_n). Helps list possible rational roots to test (using Factor Theorem or synthetic division).

Factoring (if possible, using techniques from 4.1, often after finding one root with Factor Theorem).

Numerical methods (e.g., Newton-Raphson) for approximations if exact roots are hard to find (usually beyond scope here, but can be mentioned).

3.3 Graphical Behavior of Polynomials (End Behavior, Turning Points)

The graph of a polynomial function y = P(x) = a_n x^n + ... + a_0 is a smooth, continuous curve with no sharp corners or breaks.

End Behavior: The behavior of f(x) as x → ∞ (x becomes very large positive) and as x → -∞ (x becomes very large negative). Determined by the term with the highest power, a_n x^n (the leading term).

If n is Even:

If a_n > 0 (leading coefficient positive): As x → ±∞, f(x) → +∞. (Graph rises on both left and right ends, U-shape overall).

If a_n < 0 (leading coefficient negative): As x → ±∞, f(x) → -∞. (Graph falls on both left and right ends, ∩-shape overall).

If n is Odd:

If a_n > 0: As x → ∞, f(x) → +∞ (rises right). As x → -∞, f(x) → -∞ (falls left).

If a_n < 0: As x → ∞, f(x) → -∞ (falls right). As x → -∞, f(x) → +∞ (rises left).

Intercepts:

y-intercept: Set x = 0. f(0) = a_0. (Point (0, a_0)).

x-intercepts (Roots/Zeros): Set f(x) = 0 and solve for x. These are where graph crosses/touches x-axis.

Turning Points (Local Maxima and Minima): Points where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing, or vice versa.

A polynomial of degree n has at most n - 1 turning points.

The exact location of turning points requires calculus (finding where derivative P'(x) = 0), but understanding the maximum possible number helps in sketching.

Multiplicity of Roots and Behavior at x-intercepts: (As discussed in 3.2).

Sketching Polynomials:

Determine end behavior.

Find y-intercept.

Find x-intercepts (roots) and their multiplicities (if possible).

Consider maximum number of turning points.

Plot a few additional points if needed to refine shape.

Draw a smooth, continuous curve connecting the points and exhibiting correct end behavior and behavior at roots.

3.4 Asymptotes and Graphing Rational Functions

Rational Function (Review): f(x) = P(x) / Q(x), where P(x) and Q(x) are polynomials and Q(x) ≠ 0.

Asymptotes: Lines that the graph of a function approaches but typically does not touch or cross as the independent variable (x) or dependent variable (y or f(x)) approaches infinity or a specific value where the function is undefined.

Vertical Asymptotes (VA):

Occur at values of x for which the denominator Q(x) = 0 AND the numerator P(x) ≠ 0.

If x = c is a VA, the graph will approach +∞ or -∞ as x approaches c from the left or right.

To find VAs: Set denominator Q(x) = 0 and solve for x. Check that these values do not also make P(x) = 0.

If both P(c)=0 and Q(c)=0, there might be a hole (removable discontinuity) at x=c instead of a VA (if the common factor (x-c) cancels out).

Horizontal Asymptotes (HA): Describe the end behavior of f(x) as x → ∞ or x → -∞.

Compare the degree of the numerator P(x) (let it be n) and the degree of the denominator Q(x) (let it be m):

Case 1: If n < m (degree of numerator < degree of denominator):
The HA is the x-axis, y = 0.

Case 2: If n = m (degrees are equal):
The HA is y = (leading coefficient of P(x)) / (leading coefficient of Q(x)).

Case 3: If n > m (degree of numerator > degree of denominator):
There is no horizontal asymptote.

If n = m + 1, there is a slant (oblique) asymptote. (Find by performing polynomial long division of P(x) by Q(x); the quotient y = mx + b (ignoring remainder) is the equation of the slant asymptote).

If n > m + 1, the end behavior is like a higher-degree polynomial (no simple line asymptote).

Slant (Oblique) Asymptotes (SA):

Occur when the degree of the numerator is exactly one greater than the degree of the denominator (n = m + 1).

Found by dividing P(x) by Q(x). The quotient y = ax + b (linear part) is the equation of the SA. The remainder term approaches 0 as x → ±∞.

Sketching Rational Functions:

Find Intercepts:

y-intercept: Set x=0, find f(0).

x-intercepts: Set f(x)=0 (which means set numerator P(x)=0 and solve for x).

Find Asymptotes:

Vertical Asymptotes (and check for holes).

Horizontal or Slant Asymptotes.

Determine Behavior Near Vertical Asymptotes: Test values of x slightly to the left and right of each VA to see if f(x) → +∞ or f(x) → -∞.

Determine Behavior Near Horizontal/Slant Asymptotes (End Behavior): Does graph approach from above or below?

Plot Key Points: Intercepts, points near VAs, points between intercepts/VAs.

Check for Symmetry (if any: y-axis if f(-x)=f(x), origin if f(-x)=-f(x)).

Draw Smooth Curves: Connect plotted points, approaching the asymptotes. Ensure the graph passes the vertical line test.

Chapter 4: Logarithmic and Exponential Functions

Introduction:
Exponential and logarithmic functions are fundamental in mathematics and describe many phenomena in science, engineering, finance, and nature, such as population growth, radioactive decay, compound interest, and pH scales. Exponential functions involve a constant base raised to a variable exponent, while logarithmic functions are their inverses. This chapter explores the properties of these functions, the laws governing their operations, methods for solving equations involving them, and their common applications.

Exponential Functions (Review from General Math Ch 8, focus on properties/equations):
A function of the form f(x) = b^x or y = b^x, where b is a positive constant called the base (b > 0, b ≠ 1), and x is the variable exponent.

The Natural Exponential Function: f(x) = e^x, where e ≈ 2.71828... (Euler's number) is the natural base. This is the most common base in calculus and many scientific applications due to its unique properties (e.g., d/dx (e^x) = e^x).

Properties (for b > 0, b ≠ 1):

Domain: All real numbers ((-∞, ∞)).

Range: All positive real numbers ((0, ∞)). (Graph is always above x-axis).

y-intercept: (0, 1) (since b⁰ = 1). No x-intercept.

Horizontal Asymptote: The x-axis (y = 0).

If b > 1: Exponential growth (graph increases from left to right).

If 0 < b < 1: Exponential decay (graph decreases from left to right).

One-to-one function (passes horizontal line test), so has an inverse (logarithmic function).

4.1 Laws of Logarithms, Change of Base Formula

Logarithmic Function: The inverse of an exponential function.
If b^y = x, then log_b (x) = y.
This is read as "logarithm of x to the base b is y". It answers the question: "To what power must b be raised to get x?"

b is the base (b > 0, b ≠ 1).

x is the argument of the logarithm (x > 0). The logarithm of a non-positive number is undefined in real numbers.

y is the logarithm (the exponent).

Common Logarithm: Base 10, denoted log(x) or log₁₀(x).

Natural Logarithm: Base e, denoted ln(x) or log_e(x). (ln(x) = y means e^y = x).

Relationship between Exponential and Logarithmic Forms:
b^y = x <=> log_b(x) = y

log_b(b^x) = x

b^(log_b x) = x (for x > 0)

Laws (Properties) of Logarithms: (Derived from laws of exponents)
Assume b > 0, b ≠ 1, and M > 0, N > 0. p is any real number.

Product Rule: log_b (MN) = log_b (M) + log_b (N)
(Log of a product is sum of logs).

Quotient Rule: log_b (M/N) = log_b (M) - log_b (N)
(Log of a quotient is difference of logs).

Power Rule: log_b (M^p) = p log_b (M)
(Log of a power is exponent times log of base).

Special Logarithms:

log_b (b) = 1 (since b¹ = b).

log_b (1) = 0 (since b⁰ = 1).

log_b (b^x) = x.

b^(log_b x) = x.

Change of Base Formula:
Allows conversion of logarithms from one base to another.
log_b (M) = log_a (M) / log_a (b)
(where a is any new suitable base, often base 10 or base e for calculator use).

Useful for evaluating logarithms with bases not directly available on a calculator.

log_b (M) = log(M) / log(b) (using common log, base 10)

log_b (M) = ln(M) / ln(b) (using natural log, base e)

4.2 Exponential Growth and Decay Models

Exponential functions model many real-world phenomena where a quantity increases or decreases at a rate proportional to its current value.
General Exponential Growth/Decay Model:
A(t) = A₀ e^(kt) or A(t) = A₀ b^t (where b = e^k)
Where:

A(t) = Amount or quantity at time t.

A₀ = Initial amount or quantity (at t=0).

k = Growth rate constant (if k > 0 for growth) or decay rate constant (if k < 0 for decay). Often expressed as a percentage rate r (e.g., r = 100k%).

t = Time.

e = Base of natural logarithm.

b = Growth factor (if b > 1) or decay factor (if 0 < b < 1) per unit time. b = 1 + r_decimal for growth, b = 1 - r_decimal for decay if r_decimal is rate per period.

Applications:

Population Growth (Unrestricted):

Often modeled by P(t) = P₀ e^(kt) where P₀ is initial population, k is relative growth rate.

Doubling Time: Time it takes for population to double.

Compound Interest (Continuous Compounding):

If principal P is invested at annual rate r compounded continuously, amount A after t years is A(t) = P e^(rt).

(For compounding n times per year: A(t) = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). As n → ∞, this approaches P e^(rt)).

Radioactive Decay:

The rate of decay of a radioactive substance is proportional to the amount of substance present.

N(t) = N₀ e^(-λt)
where N(t) = amount remaining at time t, N₀ = initial amount, λ = decay constant (positive). (Note the negative sign in exponent for decay).

Half-Life (t½): Time it takes for half of a radioactive sample to decay.
t½ = ln(2) / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ.
After x half-lives, fraction remaining = (½)^x.

Newton's Law of Cooling/Heating: Rate of temperature change of an object is proportional to the difference between its temperature and the ambient temperature. Leads to exponential approach to ambient temperature.

Other examples: Spread of epidemics (initial stages), drug concentration in bloodstream, atmospheric pressure with altitude (decay).

4.3 Solving Exponential and Logarithmic Equations

Exponential Equations: Equations where the variable appears in the exponent.

Strategy 1: Same Base Method
If equation can be written as b^X = b^Y, then X = Y. (Equate exponents).

Example: Solve 2^(x+1) = 8.
2^(x+1) = 2³. So, x+1 = 3 => x = 2.

Strategy 2: Taking Logarithms of Both Sides
If bases cannot be easily made the same, take log (common or natural) of both sides. Then use log properties (especially power rule log(M^p) = p log(M)) to bring variable down.

Example: Solve 3^x = 20.
log(3^x) = log(20) (or ln(3^x) = ln(20))
x log(3) = log(20)
x = log(20) / log(3) ≈ 2.727.

Example: Solve 5^(2x-1) = 10.
ln(5^(2x-1)) = ln(10)
(2x - 1) ln(5) = ln(10)
2x - 1 = ln(10) / ln(5)
2x = 1 + (ln(10) / ln(5))
x = [1 + (ln(10) / ln(5))] / 2 ≈ 1.215.

Equations involving e^x: Often best to use natural logarithm ln.

Logarithmic Equations: Equations that contain logarithmic expressions.

Strategy 1: Using Definition of Logarithm
If equation is in form log_b(X) = Y, rewrite in exponential form b^Y = X and solve for X (or the variable within X).

Example: Solve log₂(x - 3) = 4.
Rewrite: x - 3 = 2⁴
x - 3 = 16 => x = 19.
Check for Validity: Argument of log must be positive. 19 - 3 = 16 > 0. Solution is valid.

Strategy 2: Using Log Properties to Combine/Simplify
If multiple log terms, use log laws (product, quotient, power rules) to combine them into a single logarithm on one or both sides.

If log_b(X) = log_b(Y), then X = Y.

Example: Solve log₃(x) + log₃(x - 2) = 1.
log₃[x(x - 2)] = 1 (Product rule)
Rewrite in exponential form: x(x - 2) = 3¹
x² - 2x = 3 => x² - 2x - 3 = 0
(x - 3)(x + 1) = 0. Solutions: x = 3 or x = -1.
Check for Validity:
If x = 3: log₃(3) is defined, log₃(3-2) = log₃(1) is defined. So x = 3 is a valid solution.
If x = -1: log₃(-1) is undefined (argument of log must be positive). So x = -1 is an extraneous solution and must be rejected.
Only solution: x = 3.

Always check solutions to logarithmic equations by substituting them back into the original equation to ensure that arguments of logarithms are positive and bases are valid.

Compound Interest (A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) or A = Pe^(rt)):

Solving for t (time to reach a certain amount).

Solving for r (rate required).

Doubling time (time for investment to double).

Example: How long will it take for $1000 to grow to $2000 if invested at 5% per annum compounded continuously?
2000 = 1000 e^(0.05t)
2 = e^(0.05t)
ln(2) = 0.05t
t = ln(2) / 0.05 ≈ 13.86 years.

Population Models (P(t) = P₀ e^(kt)):

Solving for t (time to reach certain population size).

Solving for k (growth/decay rate) given population at two different times.

Solving for t (age of a sample in carbon dating).

Solving for λ (decay constant) or T½ (half-life).

Example: A sample of Carbon-14 (half-life 5730 years) has 20% of its original C-14 remaining. How old is it?
N(t)/N₀ = 0.20.
0.20 = (½)^(t / 5730)
Take log or ln of both sides. ln(0.20) = (t / 5730) ln(0.5)
t = 5730 × [ln(0.20) / ln(0.5)] ≈ 5730 × [-1.6094 / -0.6931] ≈ 5730 × 2.3219 ≈ 13,300 years.

pH Calculations: pH = -log[H⁺]. Can involve solving for [H⁺] = 10^(-pH).

Chapter 5: Limits, Continuity, and Differentiation

Introduction:
Calculus, developed independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the 17th century, is a powerful branch of mathematics that deals with rates of change (differential calculus) and accumulation (integral calculus). The fundamental concept underpinning all of calculus is the limit. This chapter introduces the intuitive idea of a limit, explores techniques for evaluating limits, defines continuity of functions, and then delves into differential calculus, covering the definition of the derivative, rules for differentiation, and applications such as finding tangents, rates of change, and solving optimization problems.

5.1 The Concept of a Limit and Techniques for Evaluation

Intuitive Definition of a Limit:
Let f(x) be a function. If f(x) gets arbitrarily close to a single real number L as x approaches some number c (from either side of c), but not necessarily x=c itself, then we say that the limit of f(x) as x approaches c is L.
We write: lim (x→c) f(x) = L

The limit describes the behavior of the function near a point, not necessarily at the point. The function f(c) may or may not be defined, or may be different from L.

One-Sided Limits:

Right-hand limit: lim (x→c⁺) f(x) = L (x approaches c from values greater than c).

Left-hand limit: lim (x→c⁻) f(x) = M (x approaches c from values less than c).

A two-sided limit lim (x→c) f(x) = L exists if and only if both the left-hand and right-hand limits exist and are equal to L. (L = M).

When Limits Do Not Exist:

Left-hand and right-hand limits are different.

f(x) increases or decreases without bound (approaches ±∞) as x → c. (Sometimes written as lim = ∞ or -∞, but strictly speaking, limit DNE).

f(x) oscillates infinitely as x → c (e.g., lim (x→0) sin(1/x)).

Techniques for Evaluating Limits (for well-behaved functions):

Direct Substitution: If f(x) is a polynomial, rational function (and c is in its domain), trigonometric, exponential, or logarithmic function (and c is in its domain), then lim (x→c) f(x) = f(c).

If Direct Substitution yields Indeterminate Form (0/0 or ∞/∞):

Factorization and Cancellation: Factor numerator and denominator, cancel common factors, then try direct substitution again.

Example: lim (x→2) [(x² - 4) / (x - 2)] (0/0 form)
= lim (x→2) [(x-2)(x+2) / (x-2)] = lim (x→2) (x+2) = 2+2 = 4.

Rationalizing (using Conjugates): If expression involves square roots.

Example: lim (x→0) [ (√(x+1) - 1) / x ] (0/0 form)
Multiply numerator and denominator by (√(x+1) + 1).

Simplifying Complex Fractions.

L'Hôpital's Rule (if derivatives known): If limit is of form 0/0 or ∞/∞, then lim (x→c) [f(x)/g(x)] = lim (x→c) [f'(x)/g'(x)], provided the latter limit exists.

Limits at Infinity (End Behavior): lim (x→±∞) f(x).

For rational functions P(x)/Q(x): Divide every term in numerator and denominator by the highest power of x in the denominator. Then evaluate limit as x→±∞ (terms like k/xⁿ → 0).

Compares degrees of P(x) and Q(x) (similar to finding horizontal asymptotes).

Consider dominant terms for other functions.

Limit Laws (Properties of Limits):
If lim (x→c) f(x) = L and lim (x→c) g(x) = M, and k is a constant:

Sum/Difference: lim (x→c) [f(x) ± g(x)] = L ± M

Constant Multiple: lim (x→c) [k f(x)] = k L

Product: lim (x→c) [f(x) g(x)] = L M

Quotient: lim (x→c) [f(x) / g(x)] = L / M (if M ≠ 0)

Power: lim (x→c) [f(x)]ⁿ = Lⁿ

Root: lim (x→c) ⁿ√[f(x)] = ⁿ√L (if defined).

5.2 Continuity at a Point and Intermediate Value Theorem

Continuity of a Function:
Intuitively, a function is continuous if its graph can be drawn without lifting the pen from the paper (no breaks, jumps, or holes).

Definition of Continuity at a Point x = c:
A function f(x) is continuous at a point x = c if all three of the following conditions are met:

f(c) is defined (c is in the domain of f).

lim (x→c) f(x) exists (i.e., left-hand limit = right-hand limit).

lim (x→c) f(x) = f(c) (The limit equals the function value).

If any of these conditions fail, the function is discontinuous at x = c.

Types of Discontinuities:

Removable Discontinuity (Hole): Limit exists, but f(c) is undefined or lim ≠ f(c).

Jump Discontinuity: Left and right-hand limits exist but are not equal.

Infinite Discontinuity: One or both one-sided limits are ±∞ (often at a vertical asymptote).

Continuity on an Interval: A function is continuous on an open interval (a,b) if it is continuous at every point in the interval. For a closed interval [a,b], it must also be continuous from the right at a and from the left at b.

Many common functions are continuous on their domains: Polynomials, rational functions (on their domains), exponential, logarithmic, trigonometric functions (on their domains), root functions.

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT):
If a function f(x) is continuous on a closed interval [a, b], and N is any number between f(a) and f(b) (where f(a) ≠ f(b)), then there exists at least one number c in the open interval (a, b) such that f(c) = N.

Significance: Guarantees that a continuous function takes on all intermediate values between f(a) and f(b).

A common application is to show the existence of roots of an equation: If f(x) is continuous on [a,b] and f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs (one positive, one negative), then there must be at least one root c in (a,b) where f(c) = 0.

5.3 Differentiation Rules: Product, Quotient, Chain Rule

The Derivative (Definition):
The derivative of a function f(x) with respect to x, denoted f'(x) or dy/dx or d/dx[f(x)], measures the instantaneous rate of change of f(x) with respect to x.
Geometrically, f'(c) is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of y = f(x) at the point x = c.
f'(x) = lim (h→0) [ (f(x+h) - f(x)) / h ] (Definition using h for Δx).

Basic Differentiation Rules (Power Rule, Constant Multiple, Sum/Difference - often reviewed first):

Constant Rule: d/dx (c) = 0 (where c is a constant).

Power Rule: d/dx (xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹ (where n is any real number).

Constant Multiple Rule: d/dx [c f(x)] = c f'(x).

Sum/Difference Rule: d/dx [f(x) ± g(x)] = f'(x) ± g'(x).

Derivatives of e^x and ln(x):

d/dx (e^x) = e^x

d/dx (ln(x)) = 1/x (for x > 0)

Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions:

d/dx (sin x) = cos x

d/dx (cos x) = -sin x

d/dx (tan x) = sec²x

(And derivatives of csc x, sec x, cot x).

Advanced Differentiation Rules:

Product Rule: Used to differentiate the product of two functions.
If h(x) = f(x) g(x), then h'(x) = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).
(Derivative of first × second + first × derivative of second).

Example: d/dx (x² sin x) = (2x)(sin x) + (x²)(cos x).

Quotient Rule: Used to differentiate the quotient (division) of two functions.
If h(x) = f(x) / g(x) (where g(x) ≠ 0), then h'(x) = [f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)] / [g(x)]².
(Derivative of numerator × denominator – numerator × derivative of denominator, all divided by square of denominator).

Example: d/dx (x³ / (x+1)) = [ (3x²)(x+1) - (x³)(1) ] / (x+1)² = (3x³ + 3x² - x³) / (x+1)² = (2x³ + 3x²) / (x+1)².

Chain Rule (for Composite Functions): Used to differentiate a function of a function (a composite function y = f(g(x)) or y = f(u) where u = g(x)).
If y = f(u) and u = g(x), then dy/dx = (dy/du) × (du/dx).
Or, if h(x) = f(g(x)), then h'(x) = f'(g(x)) × g'(x).
(Derivative of the outer function (with inner function unchanged) × derivative of the inner function).

Example: y = (x² + 1)³. Let u = x² + 1, so y = u³.
dy/du = 3u². du/dx = 2x.
dy/dx = (3u²)(2x) = 3(x² + 1)² (2x) = 6x(x² + 1)².

Example: y = sin(5x). Let u = 5x, y = sin u.
dy/du = cos u. du/dx = 5.
dy/dx = (cos u)(5) = 5 cos(5x).

The Chain Rule is fundamental and used very frequently, often in combination with other rules.

Implicit Differentiation: Technique used to find dy/dx when y is defined implicitly as a function of x (i.e., equation cannot be easily solved for y). Differentiate both sides of equation with respect to x, treating y as y(x), then solve for dy/dx.

5.4 Applications: Tangents, Normals, Rate of Change, Optimization

Derivatives have numerous important applications.

Finding Equations of Tangent and Normal Lines to a Curve:

The derivative f'(c) gives the slope of the tangent line to the curve y = f(x) at the point x = c.

Equation of Tangent Line at (c, f(c)): Use point-slope form y - y₁ = m(x - x₁).
y - f(c) = f'(c) (x - c).

Normal Line: The line perpendicular to the tangent line at the point of tangency.
Slope of normal m_normal = -1 / f'(c) (if f'(c) ≠ 0).
Equation of normal line: y - f(c) = [-1/f'(c)] (x - c).

Rates of Change:

Instantaneous rate of change of y with respect to x is dy/dx.

Velocity and Acceleration (Kinematics):

If s(t) is position function, v(t) = s'(t) = ds/dt (velocity).

a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) = d²s/dt² (acceleration).

Related Rates: Problems involving finding the rate of change of one quantity in terms of the rate of change of another related quantity (often with respect to time, use chain rule implicitly).

Increasing and Decreasing Functions; First Derivative Test for Local Extrema:

If f'(x) > 0 on an interval, f(x) is increasing on that interval.

If f'(x) < 0 on an interval, f(x) is decreasing on that interval.

If f'(x) = 0 or f'(x) is undefined at x=c, then c is a critical point.

First Derivative Test:

If f'(x) changes from + to - at c (inc → dec), f(c) is a local maximum.

If f'(x) changes from - to + at c (dec → inc), f(c) is a local minimum.

If f'(x) does not change sign, no local extremum.

Concavity and Points of Inflection; Second Derivative Test:

Concavity: Describes the "curvature" of the graph.

If f''(x) > 0 on an interval, graph is concave upward (U-shape) on that interval.

If f''(x) < 0 on an interval, graph is concave downward (∩-shape) on that interval.

Point of Inflection: A point where the concavity of the graph changes (where f''(x) = 0 or is undefined, and f''(x) changes sign).

Second Derivative Test for Local Extrema: If f'(c) = 0:

If f''(c) > 0, then f(c) is a local minimum.

If f''(c) < 0, then f(c) is a local maximum.

If f''(c) = 0, test is inconclusive (use First Derivative Test).

Optimization Problems: Finding the maximum or minimum values of a function subject to certain constraints. Often involves:

Setting up a function representing the quantity to be optimized.

Finding critical points by setting its derivative equal to zero.

Using first or second derivative tests (or checking endpoints of an interval) to determine maximum/minimum.

Examples: Maximizing area/volume, minimizing cost/distance, etc.

Chapter 6: Integration Techniques
Introduction:
Integral calculus is the second major branch of calculus, concerned with the concept of integration. Integration can be viewed as the reverse process of differentiation (finding an antiderivative) or as a method for accumulating quantities, such as finding the area under a curve, volume of a solid, or total change from a rate of change. This chapter introduces indefinite and definite integrals, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus which links differentiation and integration, and various techniques for evaluating integrals, along with their applications.
6.1 Indefinite Integrals: Power Rule, Substitution
Antiderivative:
If F'(x) = f(x), then F(x) is called an antiderivative of f(x).
If F(x) is an antiderivative of f(x), then F(x) + C (where C is any constant) is also an antiderivative, because d/dx (C) = 0. C is called the constant of integration.
Indefinite Integral:
The set of all antiderivatives of a function f(x) is called the indefinite integral of f(x) with respect to x, denoted by:
∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + C
where:
∫ is the integral sign.
f(x) is the integrand.
dx indicates that the variable of integration is x.
F(x) is any particular antiderivative of f(x).
C is the constant of integration.
Basic Integration Rules (derived from differentiation rules in reverse):
Constant Rule: ∫ k dx = kx + C (where k is a constant).
Power Rule (for n ≠ -1): ∫ xⁿ dx = (x^(n+1))/(n+1) + C
Example: ∫ x³ dx = x⁴/4 + C.
Example: ∫ √x dx = ∫ x^(1/2) dx = (x^(3/2))/(3/2) + C = (2/3)x^(3/2) + C.
Case n = -1 (Log Rule): ∫ (1/x) dx = ∫ x⁻¹ dx = ln|x| + C (for x ≠ 0).
(The absolute value is used because ln(x) is only defined for x > 0, while 1/x is defined for x ≠ 0).
Constant Multiple Rule: ∫ k f(x) dx = k ∫ f(x) dx.
Sum/Difference Rule: ∫ [f(x) ± g(x)] dx = ∫ f(x) dx ± ∫ g(x) dx.
Integrals of Exponential Functions:
∫ e^x dx = e^x + C.
∫ a^x dx = a^x / ln(a) + C (where a > 0, a ≠ 1).
Integrals of Basic Trigonometric Functions: (x usually in radians)
∫ cos(x) dx = sin(x) + C
∫ sin(x) dx = -cos(x) + C
∫ sec²(x) dx = tan(x) + C
∫ csc²(x) dx = -cot(x) + C
∫ sec(x)tan(x) dx = sec(x) + C
∫ csc(x)cot(x) dx = -csc(x) + C
Integration by Substitution (u-Substitution):
A technique for integrating composite functions, essentially the reverse of the chain rule for differentiation.
If an integral is of the form ∫ f(g(x)) g'(x) dx:
Let u = g(x) (the "inner" function).
Then du = g'(x) dx.
Substitute u and du into the integral: ∫ f(u) du.
Integrate f(u) with respect to u.
Substitute back g(x) for u to get the result in terms of x.
Choosing u: Often, u is an expression inside parentheses, under a radical, in an exponent, or in the denominator. Its derivative (or a constant multiple of it) g'(x)dx should also be present in the integrand.
Example: ∫ 2x (x² + 1)³ dx
Let u = x² + 1. Then du = 2x dx.
Substitute: ∫ u³ du = u⁴/4 + C = (x² + 1)⁴ / 4 + C.
Example: ∫ cos(3x) dx
Let u = 3x. Then du = 3 dx, so dx = du/3.
Substitute: ∫ cos(u) (du/3) = (1/3) ∫ cos(u) du = (1/3) sin(u) + C = (1/3) sin(3x) + C.

6.2 Definite Integrals and Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Definite Integral:
The definite integral of a function f(x) from x = a to x = b, denoted by ∫[a,b] f(x) dx, represents the net signed area between the graph of y = f(x) and the x-axis, over the interval [a, b].
a is the lower limit of integration.
b is the upper limit of integration.
Area above x-axis is positive; area below x-axis is negative.
If f(x) is non-negative on [a,b], the definite integral is the area under the curve.
The definite integral is a number, not a function plus a constant.
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC):
Connects differentiation and integration, showing they are inverse operations. It has two parts:
Part 1 (FTC1 - Derivative of an Integral):
If f is continuous on [a,b], and G(x) = ∫[a,x] f(t) dt, then G'(x) = d/dx [∫[a,x] f(t) dt] = f(x).
(The derivative of a definite integral with respect to its upper limit is the integrand evaluated at that upper limit).
Part 2 (FTC2 - Evaluation Theorem):
If f is continuous on [a,b] and F is any antiderivative of f (i.e., F'(x) = f(x)), then:
∫[a,b] f(x) dx = F(b) - F(a)
This is usually written as [F(x)]_[a,b] (or F(x) |_[a,b]).
This part provides the practical method for evaluating definite integrals.
Properties of Definite Integrals:
∫[a,a] f(x) dx = 0
∫[b,a] f(x) dx = - ∫[a,b] f(x) dx (Reversing limits changes sign).
∫[a,b] k f(x) dx = k ∫[a,b] f(x) dx
∫[a,b] [f(x) ± g(x)] dx = ∫[a,b] f(x) dx ± ∫[a,b] g(x) dx
∫[a,c] f(x) dx + ∫[c,b] f(x) dx = ∫[a,b] f(x) dx (Additivity of intervals, a<c<b).
If f(x) ≥ 0 on [a,b], then ∫[a,b] f(x) dx ≥ 0.
If f(x) ≥ g(x) on [a,b], then ∫[a,b] f(x) dx ≥ ∫[a,b] g(x) dx.
Substitution for Definite Integrals:
When using u-substitution for a definite integral, you can either:
Find the antiderivative in terms of x first, then evaluate at original x limits.
Or, change the limits of integration to be in terms of u when you substitute u and du, then evaluate directly with u limits.
If u = g(x), then u_lower = g(a) and u_upper = g(b).
∫[a,b] f(g(x)) g'(x) dx = ∫[g(a), g(b)] f(u) du.

6.3 Integration by Parts, Partial Fractions
More advanced techniques for integrating complex functions.
Integration by Parts:
The reverse of the product rule for differentiation. Used to integrate products of functions.
Formula: ∫ u dv = uv - ∫ v du
Choosing u and dv:
Identify parts of the integrand that can be u and dv (where dv must include dx and be easily integrable to find v).
A common mnemonic for choosing u is LIATE (or LATE, ILATE, DETAIL):
Logarithmic functions (e.g., ln x)
Inverse trigonometric functions (e.g., arctan x)
Algebraic (polynomial) functions (e.g., x², 3x+2)
Trigonometric functions (e.g., sin x, cos x)
Exponential functions (e.g., e^x)
Choose u to be the function type that appears first in this list. The remaining part of the integrand (including dx) is dv.
Sometimes integration by parts needs to be applied multiple times.
Example: ∫ x e^x dx.
Let u = x (algebraic) => du = dx.
Let dv = e^x dx (exponential) => v = ∫ e^x dx = e^x.
∫ x e^x dx = x e^x - ∫ e^x dx = x e^x - e^x + C.
Example (definite integral): ∫[0,π] x cos(x) dx.
Integration by Partial Fractions:
Used to integrate rational functions (P(x)/Q(x)) where the denominator Q(x) can be factored.
The idea is to decompose the complex rational function into a sum of simpler fractions (partial fractions) whose denominators are factors of Q(x). These simpler fractions are often easier to integrate.
Steps:
If degree(P(x)) ≥ degree(Q(x)), perform polynomial long division first to get a polynomial plus a proper rational fraction (degree of numerator < degree of denominator).
Factor the denominator Q(x) completely into linear factors (ax+b) and/or irreducible quadratic factors (ax²+bx+c).
For each factor in the denominator, write a corresponding partial fraction term:
Distinct Linear Factor (ax+b): A / (ax+b)
Repeated Linear Factor (ax+b)ⁿ: A₁/(ax+b) + A₂/(ax+b)² + ... + Aₙ/(ax+b)ⁿ
Distinct Irreducible Quadratic Factor (ax²+bx+c): (Ax+B) / (ax²+bx+c)
(Repeated irreducible quadratics are more complex).
Set the original rational function equal to the sum of these partial fractions.
Solve for the unknown constants (A, B, etc.) by either:
Multiplying both sides by the common denominator Q(x) and equating coefficients of like powers of x.
Or, substituting convenient values of x (e.g., roots of factors in Q(x)).
Integrate the sum of the simpler partial fractions (often involves ln|...| terms for linear denominators, or arctan for some quadratic denominators).
Example: ∫ [1 / (x(x+1))] dx.
Partial fraction decomposition: 1 / (x(x+1)) = A/x + B/(x+1).
1 = A(x+1) + Bx.
If x=0, 1 = A(1) + 0 => A=1.
If x=-1, 1 = 0 + B(-1) => B=-1.
So, ∫ [1/x - 1/(x+1)] dx = ln|x| - ln|x+1| + C = ln|x/(x+1)| + C.

6.4 Applications: Area Under Curves, Volumes of Revolution
Definite integrals have important geometric and physical applications.
Area Under a Curve (and Between Curves):
If f(x) ≥ 0 on [a, b], the area A bounded by the curve y = f(x), the x-axis, and the vertical lines x=a and x=b is:
Area = ∫[a,b] f(x) dx
If f(x) ≤ 0 on [a, b], then ∫[a,b] f(x) dx is negative. The area is |∫[a,b] f(x) dx| or -∫[a,b] f(x) dx.
If f(x) takes both positive and negative values, ∫[a,b] f(x) dx gives the net signed area. To find total geometric area, integrate positive and negative parts separately and add absolute values.
Area Between Two Curves: If f(x) ≥ g(x) on [a, b], the area A between y = f(x) and y = g(x) from x=a to x=b is:
Area = ∫[a,b] [f(x) - g(x)] dx (Top curve - Bottom curve).
If curves intersect, find points of intersection to determine limits of integration and which function is on top in each interval.
Sometimes it's easier to integrate with respect to y (∫ x dy) if functions are given as x = f(y) or if integrating horizontally is simpler.
Volumes of Revolution (Solids of Revolution):
Finding the volume of a three-dimensional solid generated by revolving a plane region about an axis (x-axis or y-axis).
Disk Method (Rotation about x-axis): If a region bounded by y = f(x) (where f(x) ≥ 0), x=a, x=b, and the x-axis is revolved about the x-axis, the volume of the resulting solid is:
Volume = ∫[a,b] π [f(x)]² dx = ∫[a,b] π y² dx
(Each disk has radius y = f(x), area πy², thickness dx).
Disk Method (Rotation about y-axis): If region bounded by x = g(y) (where g(y) ≥ 0), y=c, y=d, and y-axis is revolved about y-axis:
Volume = ∫[c,d] π [g(y)]² dy = ∫[c,d] π x² dy
Washer Method (Rotation about x-axis): If region between two curves y = R(x) (outer radius) and y = r(x) (inner radius) (R(x) ≥ r(x)) from x=a to x=b is revolved about x-axis:
Volume = ∫[a,b] π ([R(x)]² - [r(x)]²) dx
(Each washer has outer area πR², inner hole area πr², thickness dx).
(Similar for rotation about y-axis).
Method of Cylindrical Shells (Rotation about y-axis, integrating wrt x): (Sometimes simpler than washer, or if functions are easier in terms of x). If region under y = f(x) from x=a to x=b is revolved about y-axis:
Volume = ∫[a,b] 2πx f(x) dx
(Each shell has radius x, height f(x), thickness dx. Circumference 2πx).
(Similar for rotation about x-axis, integrating wrt y).
Other applications: Average value of a function, arc length, surface area of revolution, work, centroids (depending on syllabus depth).

7.1 First‐Order Differential Equations (Separable, Linear) (Continued)

Introduction to Differential Equations:
A differential equation (DE) is an equation that relates an unknown function to one or more of its derivatives. Differential equations arise naturally when modeling systems that change over time or space. The goal is often to find the function(s) that satisfy the equation.

Terminology:

Order of a DE: The order of the highest derivative present in the equation.

Degree of a DE: The power of the highest order derivative after the equation has been cleared of radicals and fractions with respect to derivatives. (Less commonly emphasized in introductory courses than order).

Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE): Involves derivatives of a function of a single independent variable (e.g., dy/dx, d²y/dt²).

Partial Differential Equation (PDE): Involves partial derivatives of a function of two or more independent variables (e.g., ∂u/∂t, ∂²u/∂x²). (Section 7.4 will give a brief overview).

Solution of a DE: A function that, when substituted into the differential equation, satisfies the equation identically for all values of the independent variable in some interval.

General Solution: Contains one or more arbitrary constants (integration constants). Represents a family of solution curves.

Particular Solution: A specific solution obtained by applying initial conditions or boundary conditions to determine the values of the arbitrary constants.

First-Order Ordinary Differential Equations:
Involve only the first derivative of the dependent variable (e.g., dy/dx = f(x,y)).
Two common types solvable by elementary methods:

Separable Equations:

A first-order DE is separable if it can be written in the form:
f(y) dy = g(x) dx or dy/dx = g(x) / f(y) or dy/dx = G(x)H(y)
This means all terms involving y and dy can be moved to one side of the equation, and all terms involving x and dx to the other side.

Method of Solution:

Separate the variables: f(y) dy = g(x) dx.

Integrate both sides: ∫ f(y) dy = ∫ g(x) dx.

Evaluate the integrals to get an implicit or explicit general solution involving an arbitrary constant C.

Example: Solve dy/dx = xy².
Separate: (1/y²) dy = x dx (for y ≠ 0).
Integrate: ∫ y⁻² dy = ∫ x dx
-y⁻¹ = x²/2 + C or -1/y = x²/2 + C.
This is an implicit general solution. We could solve for y if desired.

First-Order Linear Differential Equations:

A first-order DE is linear if it can be written in the standard form:
dy/dx + P(x)y = Q(x)
where P(x) and Q(x) are functions of x only (or constants).

Method of Solution (using an Integrating Factor, IF):

Write the equation in standard form to identify P(x).

Calculate the integrating factor (IF): IF = e^(∫ P(x) dx). (Ignore constant of integration for this step).

Multiply the entire standard form equation by the IF. The left side will then become the derivative of a product: d/dx [ (IF) ⋅ y ] = IF ⋅ Q(x).

Integrate both sides with respect to x: (IF) ⋅ y = ∫ (IF) ⋅ Q(x) dx.

Solve for y to get the general solution (including the constant of integration from the final integral).

Example: Solve dy/dx + 2xy = x.
P(x) = 2x, Q(x) = x.
∫ P(x) dx = ∫ 2x dx = x².
IF = e^(x²).
Multiply by IF: e^(x²) (dy/dx) + 2x e^(x²) y = x e^(x²).
Left side is d/dx [e^(x²) y].
So, d/dx [e^(x²) y] = x e^(x²).
Integrate: e^(x²) y = ∫ x e^(x²) dx.
(To integrate RHS, use u-sub: u = x², du = 2x dx. So ∫ x e^(x²) dx = ½ ∫ e^u du = ½ e^u = ½ e^(x²)).
e^(x²) y = ½ e^(x²) + C.
General solution: y = ½ + C e^(-x²).

7.2 Second‐Order Linear Differential Equations (Homogeneous with Constant Coefficients)

A second-order linear differential equation involves the second derivative of the dependent variable and can be written as:
a(x) (d²y/dx²) + b(x) (dy/dx) + c(x)y = g(x)
This section focuses on a simpler case:
Homogeneous Second-Order Linear DEs with Constant Coefficients:
Form: a (d²y/dx²) + b (dy/dx) + cy = 0
(or ay'' + by' + cy = 0), where a, b, c are constants, and a ≠ 0. "Homogeneous" means the right-hand side is zero.

Method of Solution (Characteristic/Auxiliary Equation):

Assume a solution of the form y = e^(mx).

Find derivatives: y' = me^(mx), y'' = m²e^(mx).

Substitute these into the DE: a(m²e^(mx)) + b(me^(mx)) + c(e^(mx)) = 0.

Since e^(mx) is never zero, divide by it: am² + bm + c = 0.
This is the characteristic equation (or auxiliary equation), a quadratic equation in m.

Solve the characteristic equation for m. The nature of the roots m₁ and m₂ determines the form of the general solution y(x):

Case 1: Two Distinct Real Roots (Δ = b² - 4ac > 0)
If m₁ and m₂ are distinct real roots, the general solution is:
y(x) = C₁e^(m₁x) + C₂e^(m₂x)
where C₁ and C₂ are arbitrary constants.

Case 2: One Real Repeated Root (Δ = b² - 4ac = 0)
If m₁ = m₂ = m (a single repeated real root), the general solution is:
y(x) = (C₁ + C₂x)e^(mx) or y(x) = C₁e^(mx) + C₂xe^(mx)

Case 3: Two Complex Conjugate Roots (Δ = b² - 4ac < 0)
If roots are complex conjugates m = α ± βi, the general solution can be written in real form as:
y(x) = e^(αx) [C₁cos(βx) + C₂sin(βx)]

Initial conditions (e.g., y(x₀) = y₀ and y'(x₀) = y'₀) are needed to find the particular solution (values of C₁ and C₂).

7.3 Applications to Physical Systems: Simple Harmonic Motion, Cooling Problems

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): (Relates to Ch 8 & 13 of Physics outline)

A common physical system exhibiting SHM is a mass m attached to a spring with spring constant k, moving on a frictionless surface (or a simple pendulum with small oscillations).

Newton's Second Law (F = ma) applied to a mass-spring system (Hooke's Law F_s = -kx for spring force, no damping):
m (d²x/dt²) = -kx
Rearranging gives: m (d²x/dt²) + kx = 0
Or, (d²x/dt²) + (k/m)x = 0.

This is a homogeneous second-order linear DE with constant coefficients. Let ω² = k/m (where ω is angular frequency).
(d²x/dt²) + ω²x = 0.

Characteristic equation: m² + ω² = 0 => m² = -ω² => m = ±ωi.
(This is Case 3 from 7.2, with α=0 and β=ω).

The general solution for displacement x(t) is:
x(t) = C₁cos(ωt) + C₂sin(ωt)
This can also be written as x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) or x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ'), where A is amplitude and φ (or φ') is phase constant. Describes oscillatory motion.

Damped Harmonic Motion: If a damping force (e.g., friction proportional to velocity, F_damp = -b(dx/dt)) is present:
m(d²x/dt²) + b(dx/dt) + kx = 0.
This is also a homogeneous second-order linear DE. The nature of its solution (underdamped, critically damped, overdamped oscillation) depends on the roots of its characteristic equation (related to values of m, b, k).

Newton's Law of Cooling (or Heating):

States that the rate of change of temperature dT/dt of an object is proportional to the difference between its own temperature T and the ambient temperature T_a of its surroundings.
dT/dt = -k(T - T_a)
where k is a positive constant (cooling/heating constant) depending on object's properties and heat transfer conditions. The negative sign indicates that if T > T_a (object hotter), dT/dt is negative (cools down).

This is a first-order separable DE. Let y = T - T_a. Then dy/dt = dT/dt.
dy/dt = -ky.
Separate: (1/y) dy = -k dt.
Integrate: ln|y| = -kt + C.
ln|T - T_a| = -kt + C.
|T - T_a| = e^(-kt + C) = e^C e^(-kt) = A e^(-kt) (where A = e^C).
T - T_a = ±A e^(-kt). Let A' be ±A.
Solution: T(t) = T_a + A'e^(-kt).
If T(0) = T₀ (initial temperature), then T₀ = T_a + A', so A' = T₀ - T_a.
T(t) = T_a + (T₀ - T_a)e^(-kt).
Describes exponential approach of object's temperature to ambient temperature.

Other applications: RL/RC/RLC circuits (electrical oscillations), population growth models (e.g., logistic growth if non-linear DEs were covered), mixing problems, chemical reaction rates.

7.4 Introduction to Higher‐Order and Partial Differential Equations (Overview)

This section provides a brief, conceptual overview for awareness; solving these is typically beyond Further/Additional Maths.
Higher-Order ODEs:
ODEs involving derivatives higher than the second (e.g., third order, fourth order).

Linear higher-order ODEs with constant coefficients can be solved using an extension of the characteristic equation method. The characteristic equation will be a higher-degree polynomial.

Used in mechanics (e.g., beam theory, vibrations of complex systems), control theory.

Systems of ODEs:
Two or more differential equations involving derivatives of two or more dependent variables with respect to a single independent variable.

Often used to model interacting populations (predator-prey models), coupled oscillators, multi-component chemical reactions.

Can sometimes be solved by converting to a single higher-order ODE or using matrix methods (eigenvalues/eigenvectors - see Ch 10).

Partial Differential Equations (PDEs):
Equations involving partial derivatives of a function of two or more independent variables.

They describe phenomena that vary in both space and time, or across multiple spatial dimensions.

Very important in physics and engineering.

Common Examples of PDEs:

Heat Equation (Diffusion Equation): Describes how temperature (or concentration) distributes over time in a region.
∂u/∂t = α (∂²u/∂x²) (1D heat equation, u(x,t) is temp, α is thermal diffusivity).

Wave Equation: Describes the propagation of waves (e.g., sound, light, vibrations of a string/membrane).
(∂²u/∂t²) = c² (∂²u/∂x²) (1D wave equation, u(x,t) is displacement, c is wave speed).

Laplace's Equation: Describes steady-state potentials (e.g., electrostatic potential in charge-free region, steady-state temperature distribution).
∇²u = (∂²u/∂x²) + (∂²u/∂y²) + (∂²u/∂z²) = 0.

Schrödinger Equation (in Quantum Mechanics): A PDE describing the wave function of a quantum system.

Solving PDEs is generally much more complex than solving ODEs and often requires advanced techniques (e.g., separation of variables, Fourier series, numerical methods).

Introduction:
Vectors are mathematical objects possessing both magnitude and direction, crucial for describing many physical quantities like displacement, velocity, force, and fields. While Chapter 2 of General Mathematics might have introduced basic 2D vectors, this chapter extends the treatment to three dimensions, delves deeper into vector operations (dot and cross products), and explores their applications in representing lines and planes, and in solving problems in geometry and physics.

8.1 Vector Addition, Scalar Multiplication, Dot and Cross Products

Vectors (Review and Extension to 3D):

Represented by arrows or in component form.

Component Form in 3D: A vector v⃗ originating from the origin can be represented by its components along the x, y, and z axes:
v⃗ = <v_x, v_y, v_z> or v⃗ = v_x î + v_y ĵ + v_z k̂
where î = <1,0,0>, ĵ = <0,1,0>, k̂ = <0,0,1> are standard unit vectors.

Magnitude (Length or Norm) of a 3D Vector:
|v⃗| = √(v_x² + v_y² + v_z²)

Position Vector: Vector from origin to a point P(x,y,z) is r⃗_P = <x,y,z>.

Displacement Vector from P₁ to P₂: P₁P₂ = r⃗₂ - r⃗₁ = <x₂-x₁, y₂-y₁, z₂-z₁>.

Vector Operations:

Vector Addition: u⃗ + v⃗ = <u_x+v_x, u_y+v_y, u_z+v_z> (Add corresponding components).
Geometrically: Tip-to-tail or parallelogram rule.

Vector Subtraction: u⃗ - v⃗ = <u_x-v_x, u_y-v_y, u_z-v_z>.

Scalar Multiplication: c v⃗ = <cv_x, cv_y, cv_z> (Multiply each component by scalar c).
Changes magnitude; reverses direction if c < 0.

Dot Product (Scalar Product):

Takes two vectors and produces a scalar quantity.

u⃗ · v⃗ = u_x v_x + u_y v_y + u_z v_z (in component form)

u⃗ · v⃗ = |u⃗| |v⃗| cos(θ) (where θ is the angle between u⃗ and v⃗, 0 ≤ θ ≤ π).

Properties:

Commutative: u⃗ · v⃗ = v⃗ · u⃗.

Distributive: u⃗ · (v⃗ + w⃗) = u⃗ · v⃗ + u⃗ · w⃗.

u⃗ · u⃗ = |u⃗|².

If u⃗ and v⃗ are orthogonal (perpendicular), then u⃗ · v⃗ = 0 (since cos(π/2) = 0).

Applications:

Finding the angle between two vectors: cos(θ) = (u⃗ · v⃗) / (|u⃗| |v⃗|).

Finding the projection of one vector onto another (Scalar projection: comp_v(u) = (u⃗ · v⃗) / |v⃗|; Vector projection: proj_v(u) = [(u⃗ · v⃗) / |v⃗|²] v⃗).

Calculating Work done by a force: W = F⃗ · d⃗.

Cross Product (Vector Product) (Mainly for 3D vectors):

Takes two vectors and produces a new vector that is perpendicular to both original vectors (and thus to the plane containing them).

Direction of u⃗ × v⃗ is given by the right-hand rule: Point fingers of right hand along u⃗, curl them towards v⃗ (through the smaller angle); thumb points in direction of u⃗ × v⃗.

Magnitude: |u⃗ × v⃗| = |u⃗| |v⃗| sin(θ) (where θ is angle between u⃗ and v⃗).
This magnitude is equal to the area of the parallelogram formed by u⃗ and v⃗.

Component Form (using determinant):
If u⃗ = <u_x, u_y, u_z> and v⃗ = <v_x, v_y, v_z>, then:
u⃗ × v⃗ = | î ĵ k̂ |
| u_x u_y u_z |
| v_x v_y v_z |
= (u_yv_z - u_zv_y)î - (u_xv_z - u_zv_x)ĵ + (u_xv_y - u_yv_x)k̂

Properties:

Anti-commutative: u⃗ × v⃗ = - (v⃗ × u⃗).

Not associative generally.

Distributive: u⃗ × (v⃗ + w⃗) = (u⃗ × v⃗) + (u⃗ × w⃗).

c(u⃗ × v⃗) = (cu⃗) × v⃗ = u⃗ × (cv⃗).

If u⃗ and v⃗ are parallel (θ=0° or 180°), then u⃗ × v⃗ = 0⃗ (zero vector). This implies u⃗ × u⃗ = 0⃗.

Applications:

Finding a vector perpendicular to two given vectors.

Calculating Torque: τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗.

Calculating Magnetic Force: F⃗_B = q(v⃗ × B⃗).

Finding the area of a parallelogram or triangle formed by two vectors.

Scalar Triple Product (u⃗ · (v⃗ × w⃗)): Volume of parallelepiped formed by three vectors.

8.2 Vector Equations of Lines and Planes (Primarily 3D)

Vectors provide a convenient way to represent lines and planes in space.
Lines in 3D:
A line in 3D is determined by a point on the line and a direction vector parallel to the line.

Let P₀(x₀, y₀, z₀) be a known point on the line, with position vector r⃗₀ = <x₀, y₀, z₀>.

Let v⃗ = <a, b, c> be a direction vector parallel to the line.

Let P(x,y,z) be any arbitrary point on the line, with position vector r⃗ = <x,y,z>.

Then the vector P₀P⃗ = r⃗ - r⃗₀ must be parallel to v⃗. So, r⃗ - r⃗₀ = t v⃗ for some scalar parameter t.

Vector Equation of a Line:
r⃗ = r⃗₀ + t v⃗
or <x,y,z> = <x₀, y₀, z₀> + t <a, b, c>
where t is a scalar parameter (t ∈ ℝ).

Parametric Equations of a Line:
By equating components:
x = x₀ + at
y = y₀ + bt
z = z₀ + ct

Symmetric Equations of a Line:
If a, b, c ≠ 0, solve parametric equations for t and equate:
(x - x₀)/a = (y - y₀)/b = (z - z₀)/c
(If one component of v⃗ is zero, say a=0, then x = x₀ and (y-y₀)/b = (z-z₀)/c).

Planes in 3D:
A plane in 3D is determined by a point on the plane and a normal vector (n⃗) perpendicular to the plane.

Let P₀(x₀, y₀, z₀) be a known point on the plane, with position vector r⃗₀.

Let n⃗ = <A, B, C> be a normal vector to the plane (non-zero).

Let P(x,y,z) be any arbitrary point on the plane, with position vector r⃗.

The vector P₀P⃗ = r⃗ - r⃗₀ lies in the plane and must be perpendicular to the normal vector n⃗.

Therefore, their dot product is zero: n⃗ · (r⃗ - r⃗₀) = 0.

Vector Equation of a Plane:
n⃗ · (r⃗ - r⃗₀) = 0 or n⃗ · r⃗ = n⃗ · r⃗₀

Scalar Equation (Cartesian Equation) of a Plane:
Substitute n⃗ = <A, B, C>, r⃗ = <x,y,z>, r⃗₀ = <x₀, y₀, z₀>:
<A, B, C> · <x-x₀, y-y₀, z-z₀> = 0
A(x - x₀) + B(y - y₀) + C(z - z₀) = 0
This can be rewritten as: Ax + By + Cz = D
where D = Ax₀ + By₀ + Cz₀.
The coefficients A, B, C in the scalar equation are the components of a normal vector to the plane.

A plane can also be determined by three non-collinear points, or by a point and two non-parallel vectors lying in the plane (can find normal vector using cross product of direction vectors).

8.3 Applications: Forces, Moments, Geometric Problems

Vectors are essential in physics for representing quantities like force, velocity, acceleration, displacement, momentum, electric and magnetic fields.

Forces as Vectors:

Resultant Force (Net Force): Vector sum of individual forces acting on an object. F⃗_net = Σ F⃗_i.

Equilibrium: If F⃗_net = 0⃗, object is in translational equilibrium.

Work Done by a Constant Force: W = F⃗ · d⃗ (dot product of force and displacement).

Moment (Torque) of a Force:

The turning effect of a force about a point or axis.

τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗, where r⃗ is the position vector from the pivot point to the point of application of force F⃗.

Magnitude τ = rF sinθ. Direction by right-hand rule.

Used in problems of rotational equilibrium and rotational dynamics.

Geometric Problems in 3D:

Angle between two lines: Angle between their direction vectors (v⃗₁, v⃗₂).
cosθ = (v⃗₁ · v⃗₂) / (|v⃗₁| |v⃗₂|).

Angle between two planes: Angle between their normal vectors (n⃗₁, n⃗₂).

Angle between a line and a plane: If θ is angle between line's direction vector v⃗ and plane's normal n⃗, then angle between line and plane is 90° - θ (or π/2 - θ).

Distance from a point to a line (in 2D or 3D).

Distance from a point to a plane.

Shortest distance between two skew lines (non-parallel, non-intersecting lines in 3D).

Determining if lines/planes are parallel, perpendicular, or intersecting.

Lines parallel if direction vectors are parallel. Planes parallel if normal vectors are parallel.

Lines perpendicular if direction vectors are orthogonal. Planes perpendicular if normal vectors are orthogonal.

Line perpendicular to plane if direction vector is parallel to normal vector.

8.4 Scalar and Vector Fields (Basic Introduction)

These concepts are more foundational for advanced physics (e.g., electromagnetism, fluid dynamics) but an introduction can be made.

Field: A physical quantity that has a value at every point in a region of space (and possibly time).

Scalar Field: Associates a scalar value with every point in space.

Represented by a function φ(x,y,z) or φ(r⃗).

No direction associated with the field value itself, only with its gradient.

Examples:

Temperature distribution in a room: T(x,y,z).

Pressure distribution in a fluid: P(x,y,z).

Electric potential field: V(x,y,z).

Density field in a non-uniform object.

Can be visualized by contour lines (2D, lines of constant scalar value, e.g., isotherms for temperature, isobars for pressure) or contour surfaces (3D).

Vector Field: Associates a vector value (magnitude and direction) with every point in space.

Represented by a vector function F⃗(x,y,z) = F_x(x,y,z)î + F_y(x,y,z)ĵ + F_z(x,y,z)k̂.

Visualized by drawing arrows at various points, where the length and direction of the arrow represent the vector field's magnitude and direction at that point.

Examples:

Gravitational field (g⃗(r⃗)): Force per unit mass.

Electric field (E⃗(r⃗)): Force per unit positive charge.

Magnetic field (B⃗(r⃗)).

Velocity field of a flowing fluid (v⃗(x,y,z,t)).

Wind velocity field in the atmosphere.

Operations on Fields (Calculus of Fields - very brief conceptual mention):

Gradient of a Scalar Field (grad φ or ∇φ):
∇φ = (∂φ/∂x)î + (∂φ/∂y)ĵ + (∂φ/∂z)k̂.
The gradient is a vector field. It points in the direction of the greatest rate of increase of the scalar field φ, and its magnitude is that maximum rate of increase. (e.g., E⃗ = -∇V).

Divergence of a Vector Field (div F⃗ or ∇·F⃗):
∇·F⃗ = (∂F_x/∂x) + (∂F_y/∂y) + (∂F_z/∂z).
The divergence is a scalar field. It measures the "outflow" or "source strength" of the vector field per unit volume at a point.

If ∇·F⃗ > 0, point is a source. If ∇·F⃗ < 0, point is a sink. If ∇·F⃗ = 0, field is solenoidal (no sources/sinks, e.g., magnetic field).

Curl of a Vector Field (curl F⃗ or ∇×F⃗):
∇×F⃗ = | î ĵ k̂ |
| ∂/∂x ∂/∂y ∂/∂z |
| F_x F_y F_z |
The curl is a vector field. It measures the "circulation" or "vorticity" of the vector field per unit area at a point (tendency to rotate a small paddle wheel).

If ∇×F⃗ = 0⃗, field is irrotational (conservative if domain is suitable, e.g., electrostatic field).

Chapter 9: Matrices and Determinants

Introduction:
A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, symbols, or expressions, arranged in rows and columns. Matrices provide a powerful tool for representing and manipulating data, solving systems of linear equations, representing linear transformations, and are widely used in various fields like computer graphics, physics, engineering, economics, and statistics. This chapter introduces the definition and types of matrices, operations on matrices, the concept of a determinant, and its applications, particularly in solving linear systems.

9.1 Definition and Types of Matrices

Matrix: A rectangular array of elements (usually numbers) arranged in m horizontal rows and n vertical columns.

A matrix with m rows and n columns is said to be an m × n matrix (read "m by n"). m × n is the order or dimension of the matrix.

Elements of a matrix A are often denoted by a_ij, where i is the row number and j is the column number.
A = [a_ij] = [ a₁₁ a₁₂ ... a₁n ]
[ a₂₁ a₂₂ ... a₂n ]
[ ... ... ... ... ]
[ a_m₁ a_m₂ ... a_mn ]

Types of Matrices:

Row Matrix (Row Vector): A matrix with only one row (1 × n).

Example: [1 5 -2]

Column Matrix (Column Vector): A matrix with only one column (m × 1).

Example: [ 3 ]
[ -1 ]
[ 0 ]

Square Matrix: A matrix where the number of rows equals the number of columns (n × n).

Main Diagonal (Principal Diagonal): Elements a_ii (from top-left to bottom-right) in a square matrix.

Zero Matrix (Null Matrix): A matrix where all elements are zero. Denoted by O.

Identity Matrix (Unit Matrix, I or I_n): A square matrix with 1s on the main diagonal and 0s elsewhere. Acts as the multiplicative identity for matrices.

Example (3×3): I₃ = [ 1 0 0 ]
[ 0 1 0 ]
[ 0 0 1 ]

Diagonal Matrix: A square matrix where all elements not on the main diagonal are zero.

Scalar Matrix: A diagonal matrix where all main diagonal elements are equal. kI.

Triangular Matrix:

Upper Triangular Matrix: A square matrix where all elements below the main diagonal are zero.

Lower Triangular Matrix: A square matrix where all elements above the main diagonal are zero.

Symmetric Matrix: A square matrix A such that A = A^T (equal to its transpose - see 9.2). a_ij = a_ji.

Skew-Symmetric (Antisymmetric) Matrix: A square matrix A such that A = -A^T. a_ij = -a_ji, and diagonal elements a_ii = 0.

9.2 Matrix Operations: Addition, Multiplication, Transpose, Inverse (2×2, 3×3)

Equality of Matrices: Two matrices A and B are equal if and only if they have the same order (m × n) and their corresponding elements are equal (a_ij = b_ij for all i, j).

Matrix Addition and Subtraction:

Can only be performed on matrices of the same order.

To add/subtract, add/subtract corresponding elements.

If A = [a_ij] and B = [b_ij], then C = A ± B means c_ij = a_ij ± b_ij.

Properties: Commutative (A+B = B+A), Associative ((A+B)+C = A+(B+C)).

Scalar Multiplication:

Multiplying a matrix A by a scalar k.

Multiply every element of A by k. kA = [ka_ij].

Matrix Multiplication (Product of Matrices):

The product AB is defined only if the number of columns in matrix A is equal to the number of rows in matrix B.

If A is an m × p matrix and B is a p × n matrix, then the product C = AB will be an m × n matrix.

The element c_ij (in row i, column j of AB) is found by taking the dot product of the i-th row of A with the j-th column of B.
c_ij = a_i₁b₁_j + a_i₂b₂_j + ... + a_ipb_pj = Σ [from k=1 to p] (a_ik b_kj)

Matrix multiplication is NOT commutative in general (AB ≠ BA).

Properties: Associative ((AB)C = A(BC)), Distributive (A(B+C) = AB + AC, (A+B)C = AC + BC). IA = AI = A.

Example:
A = [1 2], B = [5 6] (A is 1x2, B is 2x1)
[3 4] [7 8] (A is 2x2, B is 2x2 - for more illustrative example, let's assume standard 2x2 multiplication if implied by "matrix ops")

Let A = [1 2] B = [5 0]
[3 0] [0 6] (both 2x2)
AB = [ (1*5 + 2*0) (1*0 + 2*6) ] = [ 5 12 ]
[ (3*5 + 0*0) (3*0 + 0*6) ] [ 15 0 ]

Transpose of a Matrix (A^T or A'):

The matrix obtained by interchanging the rows and columns of matrix A.

If A = [a_ij] is m × n, then A^T = [a_ji] is n × m.

Properties: (A^T)^T = A, (A+B)^T = A^T + B^T, (kA)^T = kA^T, (AB)^T = B^T A^T.

Inverse of a Square Matrix (A⁻¹): (Mainly for 2x2 and 3x3 here)

If A is a square matrix, its inverse A⁻¹ (if it exists) is a matrix such that:
A A⁻¹ = A⁻¹ A = I (where I is the identity matrix of the same order).

A square matrix has an inverse if and only if its determinant is non-zero (det(A) ≠ 0). If det(A) = 0, the matrix is singular (non-invertible).

Inverse of a 2×2 Matrix:
If A = [a b]
[c d]
Then det(A) = ad - bc.
If ad - bc ≠ 0, then A⁻¹ = (1 / (ad - bc)) [ d -b ]
[ -c a ]
(Swap diagonal elements a, d; change signs of off-diagonal b, c; multiply by 1/determinant).

Inverse of a 3×3 Matrix: (More complex)
A⁻¹ = (1 / det(A)) Adj(A)
where Adj(A) is the adjugate (or classical adjoint) of A, which is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors of A. (Finding cofactors and adjugate is a multi-step process - see determinants).

9.3 Determinants: Expansion by Minors, Properties, Cramer’s Rule

Determinant of a Square Matrix (det(A) or |A|):
A scalar value associated with every square matrix. It has important properties and applications (e.g., in solving systems of linear equations, finding matrix inverse, geometric interpretation related to area/volume scaling).

Determinant of a 1×1 Matrix: If A = [a], then det(A) = a.

Determinant of a 2×2 Matrix:
If A = [a b]
[c d], then det(A) = ad - bc.

Determinant of a 3×3 Matrix (Expansion by Minors and Cofactors):
Can be calculated by expanding along any row or any column.
Let A = [a₁₁ a₁₂ a₁₃]
[a₂₁ a₂₂ a₂₃]
[a₃₁ a₃₂ a₃₃]

Minor (M_ij): The determinant of the submatrix obtained by deleting the i-th row and j-th column of A.

Cofactor (C_ij): C_ij = (-1)^(i+j) M_ij. (Sign pattern: + - +)
- + -
+ - +

Determinant (e.g., expanding along first row):
det(A) = a₁₁C₁₁ + a₁₂C₁₂ + a₁₃C₁₃
= a₁₁M₁₁ - a₁₂M₁₂ + a₁₃M₁₃
= a₁₁(a₂₂a₃₃ - a₂₃a₃₂) - a₁₂(a₂₁a₃₃ - a₂₃a₃₁) + a₁₃(a₂₁a₃₂ - a₂₂a₃₁)

(A "Sarrus Rule" diagonal method exists for 3x3 only, but expansion by cofactors is more general).

Properties of Determinants:

If a matrix has a row or column of zeros, det(A) = 0.

det(A^T) = det(A).

If two rows (or columns) are interchanged, the sign of the determinant changes.

If two rows (or columns) are identical, det(A) = 0.

If all elements in a row (or column) are multiplied by a scalar k, the determinant is multiplied by k.

If a multiple of one row (or column) is added to another row (or column), the determinant does not change. (Useful for simplifying calculations by creating zeros).

det(AB) = det(A) det(B) (for square matrices A, B of same order).

det(A⁻¹) = 1 / det(A) (if A is invertible).

Determinant of a triangular matrix (upper or lower) is the product of its main diagonal elements.

Cramer's Rule (for solving systems of linear equations Ax = b):
Provides a formula for the solution of a system of n linear equations in n unknowns, if the determinant of the coefficient matrix A is non-zero (det(A) ≠ 0).

For a system of two equations:
a₁x + b₁y = c₁
a₂x + b₂y = c₂
Coefficient matrix A = [a₁ b₁]. det(A) = D = a₁b₂ - a₂b₁.
[a₂ b₂]
Let D_x be determinant of matrix where column of x coefficients is replaced by constants: D_x = |c₁ b₁| = c₁b₂ - c₂b₁.
|c₂ b₂|
Let D_y be determinant where column of y coefficients is replaced by constants: D_y = |a₁ c₁| = a₁c₂ - a₂c₁.
|a₂ c₂|
Then, if D ≠ 0: x = D_x / D and y = D_y / D.

9.4 Applications: Solving Systems of Linear Equations

Matrices provide a powerful and systematic way to represent and solve systems of linear equations.
A system of m linear equations in n unknowns can be written in matrix form as:
AX = B
Where:

A is the m × n coefficient matrix (contains coefficients of variables).

X is the n × 1 column matrix (vector) of variables.

B is the m × 1 column matrix (vector) of constants.

Example: System
2x + 3y = 7
x - y = 1
In matrix form: [2 3] [x] =
[1 -1] [y]
(A X = B)

Methods for Solving AX = B:

Using Matrix Inverse (if A is square and invertible, i.e., det(A) ≠ 0):
If A⁻¹ exists, multiply both sides by A⁻¹ (from the left):
A⁻¹(AX) = A⁻¹B
(A⁻¹A)X = A⁻¹B
IX = A⁻¹B
X = A⁻¹B
This gives the solution vector X. (Requires calculating A⁻¹).

Gaussian Elimination (or Gauss-Jordan Elimination) using Augmented Matrix:

Form the augmented matrix [A | B] by combining the coefficient matrix A and the constant matrix B.

Use elementary row operations (EROs) to transform the augmented matrix into row-echelon form (Gaussian elimination) or reduced row-echelon form (Gauss-Jordan elimination).

EROs:

Interchange two rows.

Multiply a row by a non-zero constant.

Add a multiple of one row to another row.

Goal of Gaussian Elimination (Row-Echelon Form):

Leading entry (first non-zero number) in each non-zero row is 1 (leading 1).

Each leading 1 is in a column to the right of the leading 1 in the row above it.

All rows consisting entirely of zeros are at the bottom.

Once in row-echelon form, the system can be solved by back-substitution.

Goal of Gauss-Jordan Elimination (Reduced Row-Echelon Form):

Row-echelon form, AND

Each column containing a leading 1 has zeros everywhere else.

The solution can be read directly from the augmented matrix (if unique). [I | X_solution].

This method is general and works for any system (can reveal no solution or infinitely many solutions if A is singular or system is inconsistent/dependent).

Cramer's Rule: (Discussed in 9.3) Applicable if A is square and det(A) ≠ 0.

Types of Solutions for Systems of Linear Equations:

Unique Solution: Lines/planes intersect at a single point. (Coefficient matrix A is invertible if square).

No Solution (Inconsistent System): Lines are parallel and distinct / planes do not have a common intersection. During Gaussian elimination, leads to a row like [0 0 ... | c] where c ≠ 0 (e.g., 0x + 0y = 5, which is impossible).

Infinitely Many Solutions (Dependent System): Lines are coincident / planes intersect in a line or are coincident. During Gaussian elimination, leads to a row of all zeros [0 0 ... | 0] (a redundant equation), or more variables than independent equations. Solutions can be expressed in terms of free parameters.

Chapter 10: Introduction to Vector Spaces (Conceptual)

Introduction:
The concept of a vector, initially introduced as a geometric entity with magnitude and direction (e.g., in 2D or 3D Euclidean space ℝ², ℝ³), can be generalized to a more abstract algebraic structure called a vector space. Linear algebra is the branch of mathematics that studies vector spaces and linear mappings between them. Understanding vector spaces provides a unified framework for many concepts in mathematics, physics, engineering, and computer science. This chapter offers a conceptual introduction to vector spaces, subspaces, span, linear independence, basis, dimension, and a brief look at eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

(Note: This chapter in a "Further/Additional Mathematics" context is usually very introductory and conceptual, laying groundwork rather than delving into rigorous proofs or complex calculations of higher linear algebra.)

10.1 Definition of a Vector Space, Subspaces, Span

Definition of a Vector Space (V) over a Field (F):
A vector space V over a field F (where F is usually the set of real numbers ℝ or complex numbers ℂ) is a non-empty set of objects called vectors, on which two operations are defined:

Vector Addition (+): Takes any two vectors u⃗ and v⃗ in V and produces another vector u⃗ + v⃗ in V.

Scalar Multiplication (⋅ or juxtaposition): Takes any scalar c in F and any vector v⃗ in V and produces another vector c v⃗ in V.

These operations must satisfy ten axioms (properties) for V to be a vector space. These axioms ensure that vector addition and scalar multiplication behave in familiar and consistent ways (similar to arithmetic with real numbers and geometric vectors).
The Ten Vector Space Axioms:
Let u⃗, v⃗, w⃗ ∈ V and c, d ∈ F (scalars).

u⃗ + v⃗ ∈ V (Closure under addition)

u⃗ + v⃗ = v⃗ + u⃗ (Commutativity of addition)

(u⃗ + v⃗) + w⃗ = u⃗ + (v⃗ + w⃗) (Associativity of addition)

There exists a zero vector 0⃗ ∈ V such that u⃗ + 0⃗ = u⃗ for all u⃗ ∈ V.

For every u⃗ ∈ V, there exists an additive inverse -u⃗ ∈ V such that u⃗ + (-u⃗) = 0⃗.

c u⃗ ∈ V (Closure under scalar multiplication)

c (u⃗ + v⃗) = c u⃗ + c v⃗ (Distributivity of scalar multiplication over vector addition)

(c + d) u⃗ = c u⃗ + d u⃗ (Distributivity of scalar multiplication over scalar addition)

c (d u⃗) = (cd) u⃗ (Associativity of scalar multiplication)

1 u⃗ = u⃗ (Scalar multiplicative identity, where 1 is the multiplicative identity in F).

Examples of Vector Spaces:

ℝⁿ: The set of all n-tuples of real numbers (e.g., ℝ² - vectors in a plane, ℝ³ - vectors in 3D space), with standard component-wise addition and scalar multiplication.

The set of all m × n matrices with real entries.

The set of all polynomials of degree less than or equal to n.

The set of all real-valued functions continuous on an interval [a,b].

The set {0⃗} (containing only the zero vector) is a trivial vector space.

Subspace (W) of a Vector Space (V):
A non-empty subset W of a vector space V is a subspace of V if W itself is a vector space under the same operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication defined on V.

Subspace Test: W is a subspace of V if and only if:

0⃗ ∈ W (W contains the zero vector of V).

W is closed under vector addition: If u⃗ ∈ W and v⃗ ∈ W, then u⃗ + v⃗ ∈ W.

W is closed under scalar multiplication: If u⃗ ∈ W and c ∈ F, then c u⃗ ∈ W.

Examples:

In ℝ³, a line passing through the origin is a subspace. A plane passing through the origin is a subspace.

ℝ² is not a subspace of ℝ³ (elements are different types: pairs vs. triplets), but a plane in ℝ³ (like xy-plane where z=0) isomorphic to ℝ² is a subspace of ℝ³.

The set of all solutions to a homogeneous system of linear equations AX = 0 is a subspace (the null space of A).

Linear Combination and Span:

Linear Combination: A vector w⃗ is a linear combination of vectors v⃗₁, v⃗₂, ..., v⃗_k in a vector space V if w⃗ can be expressed in the form:
w⃗ = c₁v⃗₁ + c₂v⃗₂ + ... + c_k v⃗_k
where c₁, c₂, ..., c_k are scalars.

Span of a Set of Vectors (S = {v⃗₁, v⃗₂, ..., v⃗_k}): The set of all possible linear combinations of the vectors in S. Denoted span(S) or span{v⃗₁, ..., v⃗_k}.
span(S) = {c₁v⃗₁ + c₂v⃗₂ + ... + c_k v⃗_k | c_i ∈ F}.

The span of any set of vectors in V is always a subspace of V. It is the smallest subspace containing all vectors in S.

If span(S) = V, then the set S is said to span or generate the vector space V.

10.2 Linear Independence, Basis, and Dimension

Linear Independence and Linear Dependence:
A set of vectors S = {v⃗₁, v⃗₂, ..., v⃗_k} in a vector space V is said to be linearly independent if the only solution to the vector equation:
c₁v⃗₁ + c₂v⃗₂ + ... + c_k v⃗_k = 0⃗
is the trivial solution c₁ = c₂ = ... = c_k = 0.

If there exists at least one non-trivial solution (where at least one c_i ≠ 0), then the set S is said to be linearly dependent.

Meaning:

Linearly independent: No vector in the set can be expressed as a linear combination of the other vectors in the set. They are all "pointing in fundamentally different directions" in some sense.

Linearly dependent: At least one vector in the set can be written as a linear combination of the others (it is redundant).

Examples:

In ℝ², <1,0> and <0,1> are linearly independent.

In ℝ², <1,2> and <2,4> are linearly dependent (since <2,4> = 2<1,2>).

In ℝ³, <1,0,0>, <0,1,0>, <0,0,1> are linearly independent.

Any set containing the zero vector is linearly dependent.

Basis of a Vector Space:
A set of vectors B = {v⃗₁, v⃗₂, ..., v⃗_n} in a vector space V is called a basis for V if it satisfies two conditions:

B is linearly independent.

B spans V (i.e., span(B) = V).

A basis provides a minimal set of vectors that can be used to represent every vector in the space as a unique linear combination.

The coefficients of this linear combination are the coordinates of the vector with respect to that basis.

Standard Basis for ℝⁿ: Consists of n unit vectors e⃗₁=<1,0,...,0>, e⃗₂=<0,1,...,0>, ..., e⃗_n=<0,0,...,1>.

Dimension of a Vector Space:
If a vector space V has a basis consisting of n vectors, then n is called the dimension of V, denoted dim(V) = n.

All bases for a finite-dimensional vector space have the same number of vectors.

If V = {0⃗} (trivial space), dim(V) = 0.

dim(ℝⁿ) = n. (e.g., dim(ℝ²) = 2, dim(ℝ³) = 3).

If a vector space cannot be spanned by a finite set of vectors, it is infinite-dimensional.

Key Theorems (Stated):

If a set of m vectors spans V, and a set of k vectors in V is linearly independent, then k ≤ m.

In an n-dimensional vector space V:

Any linearly independent set of n vectors is a basis for V.

Any set of n vectors that spans V is a basis for V.

Any set of more than n vectors is linearly dependent.

Any set of fewer than n vectors cannot span V.

10.3 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors (Conceptual Overview)

This is a more advanced topic in linear algebra, often briefly introduced conceptually.
Consider a square matrix A (representing a linear transformation from a vector space V to itself).

An eigenvector of A is a non-zero vector v⃗ such that when A acts on v⃗, the result Av⃗ is simply a scalar multiple of v⃗.
Av⃗ = λv⃗

The scalar λ (lambda) is called the eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenvector v⃗.

Meaning: Eigenvectors are vectors whose direction is unchanged (or just reversed if λ < 0) by the linear transformation A; they are only scaled by the eigenvalue λ. They represent "characteristic directions" of the transformation.

Finding Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors:

The equation Av⃗ = λv⃗ can be rewritten as Av⃗ - λIv⃗ = 0⃗ (where I is identity matrix), so (A - λI)v⃗ = 0⃗.

For this equation to have a non-zero solution for v⃗ (the eigenvector), the matrix (A - λI) must be singular (non-invertible), which means its determinant must be zero:
det(A - λI) = 0.

This equation is called the characteristic equation of matrix A. Solving it for λ gives the eigenvalues.

For each eigenvalue λ found, substitute it back into (A - λI)v⃗ = 0⃗ and solve for v⃗ (this involves solving a homogeneous system of linear equations). The non-zero solutions v⃗ are the eigenvectors corresponding to that eigenvalue. (Eigenvectors are not unique; any non-zero scalar multiple is also an eigenvector).

Applications: Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are crucial in many areas:

Analyzing stability of systems (e.g., in differential equations).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) in statistics and data science.

Vibrational analysis in physics and engineering.

Quantum mechanics (eigenstates and eigenvalues of operators).

Diagonalization of matrices.

For one of the eigenvalues, the system (A - λI)v = 0 set up to show how one would find the corresponding eigenvector(s). 10.4 Applications to Differential Equations and Transformations

Linear Transformations (from ℝⁿ to ℝᵐ):

A function (mapping) T: V → W between two vector spaces V and W is a linear transformation if it satisfies:

T(u⃗ + v⃗) = T(u⃗) + T(v⃗) (additivity)

T(c u⃗) = c T(u⃗) (homogeneity) for all u⃗,v⃗ ∈ V and scalar c.

Any linear transformation T: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ can be represented by multiplication by an m × n matrix A: T(x⃗) = Ax⃗. The columns of A are the images of the standard basis vectors of ℝⁿ under T.

Matrices are thus a way to represent and work with linear transformations.

Examples: Rotations, reflections, projections, scaling, shears in ℝ² or ℝ³ can be represented by matrices.

Systems of Linear Differential Equations:

A system of first-order linear ODEs can often be written in matrix form: X'(t) = AX(t) + F(t) (where X(t) is a vector of unknown functions, A is a matrix of coefficients).

For homogeneous systems X'(t) = AX(t), solutions are often sought in the form X(t) = v⃗ e^(λt).

Substituting this into the DE leads to the eigenvalue problem Av⃗ = λv⃗.

The eigenvalues λ and corresponding eigenvectors v⃗ of the matrix A are used to construct the general solution of the system of DEs.

This is particularly useful for analyzing coupled systems (e.g., two masses connected by springs, coupled electrical circuits, predator-prey population models).

Diagonalization of Matrices (Relating to Eigenvalues/Eigenvectors):

If an n × n matrix A has n linearly independent eigenvectors, it can be diagonalized.

This means A can be written as A = PDP⁻¹, where P is an invertible matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors of A, and D is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the corresponding eigenvalues of A.

Diagonalization simplifies calculations involving powers of matrices (A^k = PD^kP⁻¹), matrix exponentials (e^A), and solving systems of linear DEs.

This chapter primarily serves as an introduction to the language and basic concepts of vector spaces and linear algebra, highlighting their power and applicability in various mathematical and scientific contexts, particularly those encountered in higher-level studies.

11.1 Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem

Conditional Probability (P(A|B)):
The probability of event A occurring, given that event B has already occurred (or is known to have occurred).
P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) (provided P(B) > 0)
Where P(A ∩ B) is the probability of both A and B occurring (intersection).

If A and B are independent events, then P(A|B) = P(A) (knowledge of B doesn't change probability of A). In this case, the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) holds.

Rearranging the conditional probability formula gives the General Multiplication Rule:
P(A ∩ B) = P(B) × P(A|B) = P(A) × P(B|A).

Law of Total Probability:
If events B₁, B₂, ..., B_k form a partition of the sample space S (i.e., they are mutually exclusive and their union is S), then for any event A:
P(A) = P(A|B₁)P(B₁) + P(A|B₂)P(B₂) + ... + P(A|B_k)P(B_k)
P(A) = Σ P(A|B_i)P(B_i)
This is useful for finding the probability of an event A by considering different mutually exclusive scenarios B_i under which A can occur.

Bayes' Theorem (Thomas Bayes):
A fundamental theorem that describes how to update the probability of a hypothesis (an event) based on new evidence. It relates the conditional probability of an event A given B to the conditional probability of event B given A.
P(A_i | B) = [P(B | A_i) P(A_i)] / P(B)
Where:

A_i are mutually exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses (events).

P(A_i) is the prior probability of hypothesis A_i (before observing evidence B).

P(B | A_i) is the likelihood of observing evidence B given that hypothesis A_i is true.

P(B) is the total probability of observing evidence B (can be calculated using Law of Total Probability: P(B) = Σ P(B | A_j) P(A_j) over all hypotheses A_j).

P(A_i | B) is the posterior probability of hypothesis A_i after observing evidence B.

Bayes' Theorem is crucial in many fields for inference, medical diagnosis, spam filtering, machine learning, etc.

For two events A and H (Hypothesis, Evidence):
P(H | E) = [P(E | H) P(H)] / P(E)
= [P(E | H) P(H)] / [P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | ¬H) P(¬H)] (if ¬H is complement of H)

11.2 Discrete Random Variables: Binomial, Poisson Distributions

Random Variable (X): A variable whose value is a numerical outcome of a random phenomenon.
Discrete Random Variable: A random variable that can take on a finite number of values or a countably infinite number of values (e.g., number of heads in 3 coin tosses {0,1,2,3}; number of defects in a batch).

Probability Distribution (or Probability Mass Function, PMF) P(X=x): For a discrete random variable X, this function gives the probability that X takes on a specific value x.

0 ≤ P(X=x) ≤ 1 for all x.

Σ P(X=x) = 1 (sum over all possible values of x).

1. Binomial Distribution: (Review and expand from General Math Ch 14.4)
Describes the probability of k "successes" in a fixed number (n) of independent Bernoulli trials (trials with only two outcomes: success or failure).

Conditions:

Fixed number of trials, n.

Each trial is independent.

Two outcomes per trial (success S, failure F).

Probability of success p is constant for each trial. Probability of failure q = 1-p.

Let X be the number of successes in n trials. X ~ Bin(n, p).

Probability Mass Function (PMF):
P(X = k) = nCk p^k q^(n-k) for k = 0, 1, 2, ..., n
where nCk = n! / [k!(n-k)!] is the binomial coefficient.

Mean (Expected Value): E(X) = μ = np

Variance: Var(X) = σ² = npq

Standard Deviation: σ = √(npq)

Applications: Quality control (number of defectives), coin tosses, number of correct answers on a multiple-choice test by guessing.

2. Poisson Distribution:
Describes the probability of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space, if these events occur with a known average rate and independently of the time since the last event.

Named after Siméon Denis Poisson.

Often used to model rare events.

Let X be the number of events in a given interval. X ~ Po(λ) or Po(μ).

Requires one parameter: λ (lambda, or sometimes μ), which is the average rate or mean number of occurrences in that specific interval. (λ > 0).

Probability Mass Function (PMF):
P(X = k) = (e^(-λ) λ^k) / k! for k = 0, 1, 2, ...
where e is Euler's number.

Mean (Expected Value): E(X) = μ = λ

Variance: Var(X) = σ² = λ (Mean equals variance for Poisson).

Adjusting λ for interval size: If average rate is given per unit time/space, and we are interested in an interval of t units, then use λ_new = λ_original_rate × t.

Applications: Number of phone calls received by a call center per hour, number of radioactive decays per minute, number of accidents at an intersection per month, number of typing errors per page, number of defects in a manufactured item.

Poisson Approximation to Binomial: If n is large and p is small in a binomial distribution (n > 20 and p < 0.05, or np < 5 are common rules of thumb), then the binomial distribution can be approximated by a Poisson distribution with λ = np.

11.3 Continuous Random Variables: Uniform, Normal, Exponential

Continuous Random Variable: A random variable that can take on any value within a given range or interval (e.g., height, weight, time, temperature).

Probability Density Function (PDF) f(x): For a continuous random variable X, this function describes the relative likelihood for X to take on a given value.

f(x) ≥ 0 for all x.

The total area under the curve of f(x) over its entire domain must be 1: ∫[-∞,∞] f(x) dx = 1.

Probability P(a ≤ X ≤ b) is the area under the PDF curve between a and b:
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫[a,b] f(x) dx.

For a continuous random variable, P(X = c) = 0 for any specific value c (area under a single point is zero). So, P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(a < X < b).

1. Uniform Distribution (Continuous):
A distribution where all values over a finite interval [a, b] are equally likely. The PDF is constant over this interval.

PDF: f(x) = 1 / (b - a) for a ≤ x ≤ b
f(x) = 0 otherwise
Graph is a rectangle.

Mean: E(X) = μ = (a + b) / 2

Variance: Var(X) = σ² = (b - a)² / 12

Applications: Random number generators (producing numbers between 0 and 1), modeling situations where any value in an interval is equally probable (e.g., waiting times if arrival is equally likely at any point in an interval).

2. Normal Distribution (Gaussian Distribution): (Review and expand from Gen Math Ch 14.4)
The most important continuous probability distribution. Bell-shaped, symmetric curve.

Defined by two parameters: mean μ and standard deviation σ (or variance σ²). X ~ N(μ, σ²).

PDF: f(x) = [1 / (σ√(2π))] e^(-(x-μ)² / (2σ²)) (complex formula, but graph shape is key).

Properties: Symmetric about mean μ. Mean = Median = Mode. Extends from -∞ to +∞.

Standard Normal Distribution (Z): A normal distribution with μ = 0 and σ = 1. Z ~ N(0, 1).

Any normal variable X ~ N(μ, σ²) can be transformed into a standard normal variable Z using the Z-score:
Z = (X - μ) / σ.

Calculating Probabilities: Use Z-tables (standard normal cumulative probability tables) or statistical software to find areas (probabilities) under the normal curve. Tables typically give P(Z ≤ z).

P(X ≤ a) = P(Z ≤ (a-μ)/σ)

P(X ≥ b) = 1 - P(X ≤ b)

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(X ≤ b) - P(X ≤ a)

Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7%): Approximately 68% of values lie within μ ± σ, 95% within μ ± 2σ, 99.7% within μ ± 3σ.

Normal Approximation to Binomial: If n is large and p is not too extreme (np > 5 and nq > 5 are common criteria), Bin(n,p) can be approximated by N(μ=np, σ²=npq). Continuity correction often used.

3. Exponential Distribution:
Describes the time between events in a Poisson process (where events occur independently at a constant average rate).

Often used to model waiting times or lifetimes of certain components.

Defined by one parameter: λ (lambda), the rate parameter (average number of events per unit time, same λ as in related Poisson process).

Or defined by θ = 1/λ, the mean waiting time.

PDF (using rate λ): f(x) = λe^(-λx) for x ≥ 0
f(x) = 0 for x < 0

Mean: E(X) = μ = 1/λ

Variance: Var(X) = σ² = 1/λ² (Standard deviation σ = 1/λ).

Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF): P(X ≤ x) = 1 - e^(-λx) for x ≥ 0.

Memoryless Property: The probability of an event occurring in a future interval is independent of how long has passed already. (P(X > s+t | X > s) = P(X > t)).

Applications: Time until next customer arrives, lifetime of electronic components (if failure rate is constant), duration of phone calls.

11.4 Expected Value, Variance, and Moment Generating Functions (Conceptual)

Expected Value (Mean) of a Random Variable (E(X) or μ):
The weighted average of all possible values of the random variable, where the weights are their respective probabilities. It represents the long-run average value if the experiment is repeated many times.

For a Discrete Random Variable X:
E(X) = μ_X = Σ [x ⋅ P(X=x)] (sum over all possible values of x)

For a Continuous Random Variable X:
E(X) = μ_X = ∫[-∞,∞] x f(x) dx

Properties of Expected Value:

E(c) = c (where c is a constant)

E(cX) = cE(X)

E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y) (for any two random variables X, Y)

E(g(X)) = Σ [g(x) P(X=x)] (discrete) or ∫ g(x)f(x)dx (continuous).

Variance of a Random Variable (Var(X) or σ²_X):
A measure of the spread or dispersion of the random variable's values around its mean. It is the expected value (average) of the squared deviations from the mean.

Var(X) = σ²_X = E[(X - μ_X)²]

Computational Formula: Var(X) = E(X²) - [E(X)]² = E(X²) - μ_X²
(where E(X²) = Σ [x² P(X=x)] for discrete, or ∫ x²f(x)dx for continuous).

Standard Deviation (σ_X): Positive square root of variance: σ_X = √Var(X). (Units are same as X).

Properties of Variance:

Var(c) = 0 (where c is a constant)

Var(cX) = c²Var(X)

Var(X + c) = Var(X)

If X and Y are independent: Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) and Var(X - Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y).

Moment Generating Function (MGF) (M_X(t) - Conceptual Introduction):
A function associated with a probability distribution that can be used to "generate" its moments (like mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis).

Defined as: M_X(t) = E(e^(tX))

For discrete X: M_X(t) = Σ [e^(tx) P(X=x)]

For continuous X: M_X(t) = ∫[-∞,∞] e^(tx) f(x) dx

Properties:

The k-th moment about the origin, E(X^k), can be found by taking the k-th derivative of M_X(t) with respect to t and then evaluating at t = 0.
E(X^k) = M_X^(k)(0) = (d^k M_X(t) / dt^k) |_(t=0)

E(X) = M_X'(0)

E(X²) = M_X''(0)

If two random variables have the same MGF (in an interval around t=0), they have the same probability distribution.

MGFs are useful for deriving distributions of sums of independent random variables (MGF of sum is product of MGFs).

Not all distributions have MGFs. (Characteristic functions are more general).

Chapter 12: Statistical Inference and Hypothesis Testing

Introduction:
While descriptive statistics summarizes data, and probability theory deals with randomness, inferential statistics uses sample data to draw conclusions (inferences) about the larger population from which the sample was drawn. This involves estimating population parameters and testing hypotheses (claims) about these parameters. This chapter will cover sampling distributions and the Central Limit Theorem (crucial for inference), construction and interpretation of confidence intervals for means and proportions, the general framework of hypothesis testing, and an introduction to common tests like z-tests, t-tests, and chi-square tests.

12.1 Sampling Distributions and Central Limit Theorem

Parameters vs. Statistics:

Parameter: A numerical characteristic of a population (e.g., population mean μ, population standard deviation σ, population proportion p). Parameters are usually unknown and are estimated.

Statistic: A numerical characteristic of a sample (e.g., sample mean x̄, sample standard deviation s, sample proportion p̂). Statistics are calculated from sample data and used to estimate parameters.

Sampling Distribution:
The probability distribution of a sample statistic (like x̄ or p̂) when samples of the same size n are repeatedly drawn from the same population.

It describes the variability of the statistic from sample to sample.

Key for understanding how well a sample statistic estimates a population parameter.

Sampling Distribution of the Sample Mean (x̄):
If we take all possible random samples of size n from a population:

Mean of the Sampling Distribution of x̄ (μ_x̄):
μ_x̄ = μ (The mean of all possible sample means is equal to the population mean). So, x̄ is an unbiased estimator of μ.

Standard Deviation of the Sampling Distribution of x̄ (σ_x̄) (Standard Error of the Mean, SEM):
σ_x̄ = σ / √n (where σ is population standard deviation, n is sample size).

Standard error decreases as sample size n increases (larger samples give more precise estimates of μ).

If σ is unknown (usually the case), it's estimated by sample SD s, and standard error is s_x̄ = s / √n.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT):
One of the most important theorems in statistics.

Statement:

If random samples of size n are drawn from any population with mean μ and standard deviation σ, then as the sample size n becomes large (n ≥ 30 is a common rule of thumb), the sampling distribution of the sample mean x̄ will be approximately normal, regardless of the shape of the original population distribution.

The mean of this approximate normal distribution will be μ_x̄ = μ.

The standard deviation (standard error) will be σ_x̄ = σ / √n.

If the original population itself is normally distributed, then the sampling distribution of x̄ is exactly normal for any sample size n.

Significance: The CLT allows us to use the normal distribution to make inferences about the population mean μ (e.g., construct confidence intervals, perform hypothesis tests) even if the original population distribution is not normal, provided the sample size is large enough.

12.2 Confidence Intervals for Means and Proportions

A confidence interval (CI) provides a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter, based on sample data, with a specified level of confidence.
Form: Point Estimate ± Margin of Error

1. Confidence Interval for a Population Mean (μ):

Case 1: Population Standard Deviation σ is known, or sample size n is large (n ≥ 30, so CLT applies and s ≈ σ):
Use Z-distribution (standard normal).
CI = x̄ ± Z*_α/₂ (σ / √n)
Where:

x̄ = sample mean.

σ = population standard deviation.

n = sample size.

Z*_α/₂ = critical Z-value for confidence level 1 - α.

For 90% confidence (α=0.10), Z*_0.05 = 1.645.

For 95% confidence (α=0.05), Z*_0.025 = 1.96.

For 99% confidence (α=0.01), Z*_0.005 = 2.576.

Case 2: Population Standard Deviation σ is unknown, and sample size n is small (n < 30):
Use t-distribution (Student's t-distribution), provided the population is approximately normally distributed.
CI = x̄ ± t*_α/₂ (s / √n)
Where:

s = sample standard deviation.

t*_α/₂ = critical t-value for confidence level 1 - α with degrees of freedom (df) = n - 1. (Found from t-distribution table).

The t-distribution is wider (more spread out) than Z-distribution, especially for small n, reflecting increased uncertainty when σ is estimated by s. As n gets large, t-distribution approaches Z-distribution.

2. Confidence Interval for a Population Proportion (p):
Used for categorical data where we want to estimate the proportion of successes in a population.

Point estimate: p̂ = x/n (sample proportion, x = number of successes in sample of size n).

Conditions for using normal approximation (Z-interval): np̂ ≥ 10 and nq̂ ≥ 10 (where q̂ = 1 - p̂). (Some texts use np ≥ 5, nq ≥ 5).

CI = p̂ ± Z*_α/₂ √[ p̂(1-p̂) / n ]
Standard error of p̂ is √[ p̂(1-p̂) / n ].

Interpretation of Confidence Intervals:
"We are [confidence level]% confident that the true population [parameter name] lies within the interval (lower bound, upper bound)." It means that if we were to take many random samples and construct many such intervals, about [confidence level]% of these intervals would contain the true population parameter.

Determining Sample Size (n) for Estimating Mean or Proportion:
Formulas can be derived to estimate the sample size needed to achieve a desired margin of error at a specific confidence level.

12.3 Hypothesis Tests (z‐Test, t‐Test, Chi‐Square Test)

Hypothesis Testing: A formal statistical procedure for making decisions about a population parameter based on sample evidence.
Steps in Hypothesis Testing (General Framework):

State Hypotheses:

Null Hypothesis (H₀): Statement of no effect or no difference, or that parameter equals a specific value. Assumed true until evidence suggests otherwise.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ): Statement contradicting H₀; what the researcher is trying to find evidence for. Can be one-tailed (< or >) or two-tailed (≠).

Set Significance Level (α): The probability of making a Type I error (rejecting H₀ when it is true). Common values: α = 0.05 (5%), 0.01, 0.10. This defines the "rejection region(s)".

Choose Appropriate Test Statistic and Check Assumptions: Select a test based on parameter being tested, data type, sample size, known/unknown population parameters (e.g., Z-test, t-test, χ²-test). Verify any assumptions required for the test.

Calculate the Test Statistic: A value computed from sample data that measures how far the sample estimate is from the value specified by H₀, in standardized units.

Determine Critical Value(s) and Rejection Region(s) OR Calculate p-value:

Critical Value Approach: Find critical value(s) from statistical tables based on α and test type (one/two-tailed). If test statistic falls in rejection region (beyond critical value), reject H₀.

p-value Approach: Calculate p-value – the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme as or more extreme than the one obtained, assuming H₀ is true.

Make a Decision:

If test statistic is in rejection region OR if p-value ≤ α: Reject H₀. Conclude there is statistically significant evidence for H₁.

If test statistic is not in rejection region OR if p-value > α: Fail to reject H₀. Conclude there is not enough evidence to support H₁ (does not mean H₀ is proven true).

Interpret the Conclusion in the Context of the Problem.

Common Hypothesis Tests:

Z-Test for Population Mean (μ): (Large sample n≥30 or σ known, population approx. normal or CLT applies).

H₀: μ = μ₀. Test statistic: Z_calc = (x̄ - μ₀) / (σ/√n).

t-Test for Population Mean (μ): (Small sample n<30, σ unknown, population approx. normal).

H₀: μ = μ₀. Test statistic: t_calc = (x̄ - μ₀) / (s/√n), with df = n-1.

Z-Test for Population Proportion (p): (Large sample: np₀ ≥ 10 and nq₀ ≥ 10 where p₀ is hypothesized proportion).

H₀: p = p₀. Test statistic: Z_calc = (p̂ - p₀) / √[p₀(1-p₀)/n].

Chi-Square (χ²) Test: Used for categorical data.

Goodness-of-Fit Test: Tests if observed sample frequencies for categories match expected frequencies from a hypothesized population distribution.
χ²_calc = Σ [(Observed Frequency - Expected Frequency)² / Expected Frequency]
Degrees of freedom df = (number of categories - 1).

Test for Independence (Association) between Two Categorical Variables: Tests if two categorical variables are independent or associated. Uses data in a two-way contingency table.
Expected frequency for each cell = (Row Total × Column Total) / Grand Total.
χ²_calc = Σ [(O - E)² / E] for all cells.
df = (number of rows - 1) × (number of columns - 1).

Compare χ²_calc to critical χ² value from table for given α and df. If χ²_calc > χ²_crit, reject H₀ (variables are not independent, or observed doesn't fit expected).

12.4 Linear Regression and Correlation Coefficients (Building on Gen Math)

(This expands on Gen Math Ch 15.4 with more formal aspects, assuming a statistics focus here).
Correlation (Review):
Measures strength and direction of linear association between two quantitative variables x and y.

Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r):
r = [ Σ(x_i - x̄)(y_i - ȳ) ] / [ √Σ(x_i - x̄)² ⋅ √Σ(y_i - ȳ)² ]
or (computational formula):
r = [nΣxy - (Σx)(Σy)] / √[ (nΣx² - (Σx)²) (nΣy² - (Σy)²) ]

-1 ≤ r ≤ +1.

r close to +1: Strong positive linear correlation.

r close to -1: Strong negative linear correlation.

r close to 0: Weak or no linear correlation.

Correlation does NOT imply causation.

Linear Regression:
Finds the equation of the straight line (line of best fit) that best describes the linear relationship between x (independent/predictor variable) and y (dependent/response variable).

Least-Squares Regression Line: ŷ = b₀ + b₁x (or ŷ = a + bx)
Minimizes Σ(y_i - ŷ_i)² (sum of squared residuals).

Slope (b₁ or b):
b₁ = r (s_y / s_x) = [nΣxy - (Σx)(Σy)] / [nΣx² - (Σx)²]
(Represents average change in y for one unit increase in x).

Y-intercept (b₀ or a):
b₀ = ȳ - b₁x̄
(Predicted value of y when x = 0; may not be meaningful if x=0 is outside data range).

Coefficient of Determination (r² or R²):
r² = (Pearson's r)². (0 ≤ r² ≤ 1).
The proportion of the total variance in y that is "explained" or "accounted for" by the linear relationship with x through the regression line.

e.g., r² = 0.81 means 81% of the variation in y is explained by x.

Making Predictions:

Use the regression equation ŷ = b₀ + b₁x to predict y for a given x.

Interpolation: Predicting within the range of the observed x values (generally more reliable).

Extrapolation: Predicting outside the range of observed x values (less reliable, use with extreme caution as linear trend may not continue).

Hypothesis Test for Significance of Correlation (or Slope):
Often, one tests if the population correlation coefficient ρ (rho) is significantly different from zero (or if population slope β₁ is different from zero).

H₀: ρ = 0 (no linear correlation) vs. H₁: ρ ≠ 0.

Test statistic (for slope, or for correlation related to t-dist): t = r √[(n-2)/(1-r²)] with df = n-2.
(This level of detail might be beyond scope; often interpretation of r and r² is the focus).
