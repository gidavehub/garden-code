Passage I
Cohesion is the grammatical and lexical linking within a text that holds it together. It is achieved through specific linguistic tools called cohesive devices. One common device is the use of transitional words like 'however' or 'therefore', which signal the logical relationship between ideas. Another is the use of pronouns, where words like 'it' or 'they' refer back to previously mentioned nouns, creating a smooth flow and avoiding repetition. Parallelism, the use of similar grammatical structures for related ideas, also contributes to cohesion by creating rhythm and emphasizing connections. For example, changing the sentence "She likes reading, writing, and to paint" to "She likes reading, writing, and painting" creates a more cohesive and pleasing structure. Ultimately, these devices ensure a text is not just a random collection of sentences but a unified whole.

The distinction between countable and uncountable nouns is crucial in English. Countable nouns refer to things that can be counted as individual units and thus have plural forms, such as 'book' and 'books'. They can be used with numbers and quantifiers like 'a', 'many', or 'several'. Uncountable nouns, on the other hand, refer to things seen as a mass or a whole—substances, concepts, or qualities—that cannot be easily separated and counted. Words like 'water', 'information', and 'furniture' fall into this category. They are not typically used in the plural and take quantifiers like 'much', 'a little', or 'a piece of'. A common mistake for learners is to say "informations" or "furnitures." Some nouns, like 'light' or 'paper', can be both, but their meaning changes: 'There is a lot of light' (uncountable) versus 'There are two lights in the room' (countable lamps).

Morphemes, the smallest meaningful units in a language, can be classified as either derivational or inflectional. The distinction is fundamental. Inflectional morphemes do not create new words; they simply provide grammatical information for an existing word, adapting it to a sentence. English has only a handful of them, all of which are suffixes, such as the plural ‘-s’ (cats), the past tense ‘-ed’ (walked), and the comparative ‘-er’ (taller). Section E: Comprehension and Mixed Practice

Instructions: Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions that follow.

When words are spoken fluently, their pronunciation often changes due to the influence of neighbouring sounds. This phenomenon, known as connected speech, includes several processes. One is assimilation, where a sound becomes more like an adjacent one for ease of articulation. For example, in rapid speech, the phrase 'ten boys' might sound like 'tem boys' because the alveolar /n/ sound assimilates to the bilabial quality of the following /b/ sound. Another process is elision, which is the omission of a sound. This commonly happens to the /t/ and /d/ sounds when they are between two other consonants, such as in 'last night', which can be pronounced 'las night'. Finally, linking involves the smooth connection of words, often by attaching a final consonant to a following vowel, as in 'an apple', which sounds like 'a napple'. Understanding these features is key to comprehending natural, rapid speech.

Explanation: The passage clearly introduces the concept of connected speech and then defines and provides examples for assimilation, elision, and linking.
